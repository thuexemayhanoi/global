Riding a Scooter in Hanoi During Heavy Rain

A practical guide to riding a rental scooter in Hanoi's rainy season: when the roads flood, how wet-weather grip works, what gear to carry, and when the smart move is to stop and wait.

Rent a scooter in Hanoi between May and October and rain becomes part of the deal. The good news: the city rarely sees all-day drizzle. The wet season here works in bursts, heavy showers that flood an intersection, soak you to the skin, and then disappear while the pavement steams dry. Riders who understand the rhythm of the season, and who know their own limits in it, get around Hanoi comfortably all summer. This guide covers the season itself, the specific hazards of wet-weather riding, flooded streets, gear, and how to keep a rental scooter healthy through months of rain.

Hanoi's rainy season at a glance

The rainy season in Hanoi runs roughly from May to October and peaks in July and August, when monthly rainfall reaches an average of 250 to 350 millimeters (Vietnam Tourism). Across the year the city collects about 1,600 millimeters of rain, with the heaviest months delivering more than 250 millimeters each (Climates to Travel).

Those numbers describe water volume, not riding conditions. What matters on a scooter is the daily pattern: mornings are usually dry, the heat builds through midday, and the first serious showers arrive in the afternoon or early evening. Rain is also strikingly local. One district can get a cloudburst while another stays dry, which is why experienced riders treat a citywide forecast as a rough first signal and check conditions again before setting off (Rentabike Vietnam). Practical takeaway: put the longer rides in the morning, keep the afternoon flexible, and never plan a tight schedule that leaves no room to wait out a shower.

The first ten minutes of rain are the most dangerous

Wet roads do not become uniformly slippery. After several dry days, the surface of a Hanoi street carries a mix of dust, oil droplets and rubber residue. The first rain lifts this into a thin, greasy film that sits on the asphalt until more rain washes it away. In those opening minutes, grip is at its worst, and that is exactly when riders who dismiss a drizzle as harmless get caught out.

On top of that film come the hard, painted and metal surfaces that fill the city: direction arrows, crosswalk stripes, lane markings, and the steel covers of drains and utility shafts. In the dry they are invisible to your riding. In the rain they behave almost like ice under a scooter tire. The rule for the first phase of any shower is therefore simple: ride visibly slower than usual, brake earlier and gentler than feels necessary, and cross painted lines and metal covers upright, with steady speed and no sudden inputs. Construction zones deserve the same respect: wet mud, gravel and loose sand are as treacherous as the first rain film.

When the sky opens, the correct move is to stop

There is a distinction every rainy-season rider in Vietnam learns quickly: light rain means slow down and keep going, a genuine downpour means pull over and wait (Rentabike Vietnam. A typical Hanoi storm spends its energy in twenty to forty minutes. A cafe awning, a covered bus stop or a shop entrance is all the shelter you need. Sitting out one storm costs you half an hour. Riding through it costs you visibility, grip, and composure you will want later in the day.

Choose your shelter with a little care. Solid roofing beats a lone tall tree, which brings a small but real risk of lightning and breaking branches. If you are already tired after a long day in the saddle, treat the first heavy shower as a mandatory stop rather than an optional one: wet gear, a fogged visor and fatigue together form the worst combination a rider faces all week.

Flooded streets hide more than water

Heavy rain finds the low points of Hanoi fast. Intersections with weak drainage, underpasses and sunken road sections fill first, and the same streets flood again and again: Nguyễn Khuyến, Đê La Thành and Minh Khai are repeatedly named among the city's flood-prone locations (Vietnam Tourism). If you are anywhere near one of these when a storm hits, getting off the main flow early is smarter than pushing through.

The water itself is not the biggest problem. Floodwater conceals potholes, broken road edges, open drain covers and debris, and its depth changes from one meter to the next. That the scooter in front of you made it through says nothing about the patch of road in front of you. Whether the water is safe to approach is a decision you make before you reach it, and never one you make by copying other vehicles (Rentabike Vietnam). Even shallow water flowing across the road can push a scooter sideways, and a scooter offers less clearance and stability than it looks.

So judge depth from a fixed reference point, a kerb, a parked bike, a signpost, before you commit. If the water hides the road surface or reaches well above your floorboard, turn around and take the detour. If the engine stalls in water, do not restart it in place; push the scooter out first and let it settle before trying the starter again.

Gear that keeps the rain manageable

Nothing about riding in the wet season requires special equipment, but a few cheap items change the whole experience. A generously cut poncho covers your torso, arms and thighs without catching wind or drifting toward the wheels. Quick-drying clothes beat cotton, which soaks through and then dries slowly in the humid air. Waterproof luggage protection, a dry bag for documents and electronics, and a touch-friendly waterproof pouch for your phone keep the important things functional. Footwear matters more than people expect: wet, smooth soles slip on floorboards and footpegs, so closed shoes with a grippy sole outperform sandals on rainy days. Rainy-season packing guides for Vietnam recommend exactly this combination of poncho, quick-dry clothing, proper footwear and waterproof luggage (Rentabike Vietnam).

Riding technique: smooth beats fast

One wet-weather rule is law rather than advice. Under Article 7, clause 1, point g of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, motorbikes and scooters must use their lights between 18:00 and 06:00 and whenever fog, smoke, dust, rain or other bad weather limits visibility; riding without lights carries a fine of 200,000 to 400,000 VND under the same decree. In rain the light is mostly for everyone else, who otherwise cannot see you. The UK Foreign Office's Vietnam travel advice is blunt about how serious road accidents are for visitors, and a downpour brings dusk-level visibility into the middle of the afternoon. If something does go wrong and someone is hurt, the numbers are 115 for an ambulance and 113 for the police, as THƯ VIỆN PHÁP LUẬT explains.

Rain rewards riders who trade speed for smoothness. Lengthen your following distance so you can brake early instead of hard. Finish braking before corners, not inside them, and apply both brakes progressively rather than stabbing the front lever. Treat painted lines, metal covers and wet brickwork as zero-grip zones: cross them upright, at steady speed, with no throttle or brake changes.

Do not charge through standing water. What looks like a shallow puddle may be hiding a pothole or an open drain, and the spray robs you of the little visibility you have left. In traffic, assume every car and bus will brake or swerve less predictably than usual, and stay out of the blind spots of large vehicles. If your visor or glasses fog up, open the visor a crack; the airflow restores more vision than it costs in comfort. None of this is advanced technique. It is the same riding you already do, done a little slower and a lot more deliberately.

Protect the scooter you rented

Rain changes what you should check at pickup. Tire tread, both brakes, lights and horn matter every day, but in the wet season they matter double, so walk through them before you accept the keys. Our scooter handover checklist for Hanoi lists exactly what to inspect (/en/checklist/).

Ask the rental shop how it handles problems in bad weather: is there a hotline that answers, does a swap happen, and how far will support go? Reliable shops answer this before you ask; a general overview of renting in the city is in our main guide (/en/).

Mechanically, keep water away from the engine's air intake, which sits low on many scooters, and treat deep water as a genuine engine risk rather than a splash. After riding through heavy rain, give the brakes a few gentle applications in a safe stretch of road; wet brakes need a moment before they bite fully. And if a ride ends completely soaked, park the scooter under cover for the night rather than leaving it standing in the rain.

After the storm

When the shower passes, the city returns fast, and so should your attention. Painted surfaces and metal covers stay slippery long after the rain stops. Brakes drag a little until they dry. Puddles shrink but keep hiding the holes that caused them. Ride the first dry hour like it is still wet, and the rest of the day like normal. That single habit, more than any gear choice, is what separates riders who finish the rainy season without stories of falls from those who do not.
