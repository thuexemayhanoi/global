110cc, 125cc or 150cc: choosing an engine size for Hanoi and the mountains

Choosing a 110cc, 125cc or 150cc-class bike in Vietnam: what extra power buys, why speed limits cap it, which licence class each needs, and how passengers, weight and fuel range change the answer.

Rental listings in Hanoi often sort bikes by engine size, and it is tempting to assume that bigger is better. It is not that simple. A larger engine gives you more pulling power on a steep climb with a passenger, but Vietnam's speed limits cap how much of it you can use, the licence class changes above 125 cm³ (Article 57(1) of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety), and the extra power matters far less in city traffic than on a mountain loop. This guide compares the three common sizes using Honda Vietnam's published figures, so you can match the bike to your route rather than to the biggest number. For the wider choice between bike types, start with our hub on choosing a rental motorbike.

What the sizes look like on real bikes

Three popular Honda models show the spread. In this guide, "150cc" stands for the 150 to 160 cm³ class, represented here by the Air Blade 160.

  • About 110 cc: the Wave Alpha 110 has a 109.2 cm³ engine with 6.12 kW, a four-speed rotary gearbox and a weight of 96 kg (Honda Vietnam, Wave Alpha). The Vision scooter has a 109.5 cm³ engine and weighs about 93 kg (Honda Vietnam, Vision).
  • About 125 cc: the LEAD ABS has a 124.8 cm³ engine with 8.22 kW, a belt drive and a weight of 114 kg (Honda Vietnam, LEAD ABS).
  • About 150 cc: the Air Blade 160 has a 156.9 cm³ engine with 11.2 kW, a belt drive and a weight of 113 kg (Honda Vietnam, Air Blade).

One detail stands out: on Honda's figures, the 160 cc Air Blade is no heavier than the 125 cc LEAD. Moving up a size does not automatically mean a heavier bike. The models themselves are compared in our guide to the Honda Wave, Vision, Air Blade and Lead.

What the extra cubic centimetres buy

Dividing Honda's power figures by the bike's weight gives a rough sense of the difference. This is our own calculation and ignores the rider: about 64 W per kilogram for the Wave Alpha, about 72 for the LEAD and about 99 for the Air Blade 160. In plain terms, the 160 has roughly half as much power again per kilogram as the 110.

That margin matters most when the load grows. A rider, a passenger and two bags can easily weigh more than the bike, and every machine then has less in reserve. On a long, steep climb the smaller engine is the first to feel it: you open the throttle further, the bike slows on the steepest ramps, and pulling away on a slope takes more care. A stronger engine keeps more in hand in the same situation. On flat city streets the difference is barely noticeable.

Speed limits cap what you can use

More power does not buy more legal speed. Under Article 6 of Circular 38/2024/TT-BGTVT, motorbikes may ride at up to 60 km/h on dual carriageways and multi-lane one-way roads in built-up areas and 50 km/h on other urban roads; outside built-up areas the ceilings are 70 km/h and 60 km/h. Motorbikes are barred from expressways altogether under Article 25(3) of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety. Every bike in this guide can reach those limits on level ground. What the larger engine changes is how easily it gets there with a load, and how much it slows on a climb. Our guide to motorbike speed limits has the full rules.

The licence class changes above 125 cm³

Vietnamese law sets the licence class by engine size, not by transmission. Under Article 57(1) of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety, class A1 covers two-wheel motorbikes up to 125 cm³ (or up to 11 kW for an electric motor) and class A covers anything larger. On Honda's figures, the Wave Alpha, the Vision and the LEAD fall within A1, while the Air Blade 160, at 156.9 cm³, needs class A. Visitors riding on a foreign licence need the matching motorcycle category on their documents; see our guides to foreign driving licences and International Driving Permits.

Getting this wrong is costly. Riding a motorbike up to 125 cm³ without a valid licence is fined VND 2,000,000 to 4,000,000 under Article 18(5)(a) of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP. On a bike over 125 cm³, holding a licence that does not match the vehicle is fined VND 6,000,000 to 8,000,000 under Article 18(7)(a), the same band as riding with no licence at all under Article 18(7)(b).

In the city: why 110 to 125 cc is usually enough

Inside Hanoi you rarely ride faster than the built-up limits, and traffic often holds you well below them. Here a lighter, smaller bike has real advantages:

  • Easier to handle: at about 93 to 96 kg, the Vision and the Wave are lighter to push into a parking space or turn around by hand than the 113 to 114 kg of the LEAD and the Air Blade 160 (Honda figures).
  • Enough power: pulling away from lights and keeping up with the flow does not need a large engine.
  • Licence: everything up to 125 cm³ stays within class A1 (Article 57(1)).

If you want more storage for shopping or a second helmet, a 125 cc scooter like the LEAD is a practical middle ground. Our guide to Hanoi traffic for first-time riders explains how the flow works.

On mountain loops: when the bigger engine helps

Out of the city the picture changes. Long climbs with a passenger and luggage are where an engine in the 150 cc class earns its keep, provided you hold the right licence (Article 57(1)).

That does not rule out a small bike. A light geared bike can climb steep roads if you choose a low gear early. Honda's Wave Alpha manual notes that engine braking slows the bike when you close the throttle and that you shift down to slow further, and it advises combining engine braking with intermittent use of the brakes on long, steep descents instead of braking continuously (Honda Vietnam, Wave Alpha owner's manual). For scooters with a belt drive, controlled braking matters even more; our guide on how to brake on a scooter covers technique, and riding bends and hairpins covers mountain corners.

Two more details are worth checking on the bike you are offered. Front-wheel ABS is fitted on the Air Blade 160 and on the Special version of the LEAD ABS, according to Honda (Honda Vietnam, Air Blade, LEAD ABS). And wheel sizes differ a lot: 17 inches on the Wave Alpha, 14 inches on the Air Blade, 12 at the front and 10 at the rear on the LEAD. Larger wheels tend to roll over potholes and broken edges more calmly, which counts on rough rural roads.

Fuel and range

Tank sizes are modest on every bike here: 3.7 litres on the Wave Alpha, 4.4 litres on the Air Blade 160 and 6.0 litres on the LEAD ABS (Honda figures). Honda gives the Wave Alpha a consumption of 1.72 litres per 100 km (Honda Vietnam, Wave Alpha), which works out on paper at a little over 200 km per tank. Climbs, a passenger and luggage push real consumption up, so in remote areas fill up whenever the gauge reaches half. Honda's pages do not give comparable figures for the other two models here, so ask about the exact bike before a long day.

Which size suits which trip

  • Solo, mostly in the city, first time on a scooter: a 110 cc automatic such as the Vision. Our first-ride practice plan helps you start.
  • City plus day trips, more storage, occasional passenger: a 125 cc scooter such as the LEAD.
  • Solo on mountain loops, light luggage, happy to use gears: a 110 cc geared bike such as the Wave Alpha.
  • Two-up mountain loops with luggage, experienced rider with a class A licence: a 150 cc-class bike such as the Air Blade 160 (Article 57(1)).

When in doubt, choose the smaller bike you can handle confidently. A rider in control of a modest bike is safer than one struggling with more power than they need. Our guide on manual versus automatic rentals covers the other half of the decision.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 110cc enough for the northern mountain loops? It can be, if you ride solo with light luggage, pick low gears early on climbs and use engine braking on descents. A passenger and heavy bags make a stronger engine noticeably more comfortable.

Do I need class A for a 150cc scooter? Yes. Anything over 125 cm³ needs class A (Article 57(1)), and on Honda's figures the Air Blade 160 has 156.9 cm³.

Does a bigger engine mean higher speed limits? No. The same limits apply to every motorbike, whatever its engine size (Circular 38/2024/TT-BGTVT, Article 6).

Summary

For Hanoi itself, 110 to 125 cc is usually all you need, stays within licence class A1 and is easier to handle. The 150 cc class earns its place on long, loaded mountain climbs, but it requires class A and buys no extra legal speed (Article 57(1), Circular 38/2024/TT-BGTVT). Whatever you choose, check the actual bike before you ride with our pickup checklist.
