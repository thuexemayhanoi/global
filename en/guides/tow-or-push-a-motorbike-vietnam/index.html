Towing or Pushing a Motorbike in Vietnam

Can you tow a broken-down motorbike with another bike or push it along with your foot in Vietnam? What the law says, what it costs and the safe alternatives when a rental bike stops.

The tank is empty or the engine has died, and the nearest repair shop is two kilometres away. In Vietnam you will often see the local fix: a second motorbike rides alongside and its rider pushes the broken-down bike along with one foot, or a rope is tied between the two. If you are stuck with a rental bike, it is tempting to do the same. The short answer is that the law forbids both. This guide explains what the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety says, what Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP charges for it and what to do instead. For an overview of riding safely, see our topic page on safer riding and breakdown support.

What the law forbids

Article 33(3)(đ) of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety bans motorbike and moped riders from using their vehicle to tow or push other vehicles or objects. Passengers are covered too: under Article 33(4)(c) they may not hold on to, tow or push other vehicles. Between them, these rules catch both familiar methods, the tow rope and the foot push from a moving bike.

The reasons are easy to see. A rider pushing another bike with one foot has only one foot left for balance, brakes less effectively and steers with reduced control. A rope can snap tight, break or wind into a wheel. Each bike reacts to every move of the other, and in dense traffic one pothole is enough to bring both down.

What it costs

Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP turns the ban into these penalties in Article 7:

  • Holding on to, towing or pushing other vehicles, as rider or passenger: 600,000 to 800,000 VND under Article 7(3)(e) (Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP).
  • Pulling another vehicle or object behind your motorbike: 600,000 to 800,000 VND under Article 7(3)(g) (Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP).
  • If this causes an accident: 10,000,000 to 14,000,000 VND under Article 7(10)(b), plus a 10-point deduction under Article 7(13)(d) (Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP).

What to do instead

A broken-down bike is annoying but usually sorted out quickly. These steps work:

1. Get off the roadway. Walk the bike to the right-hand edge or a safe spot beside the road. If it still blocks part of the roadway, Article 18(7) of the law requires you to warn others with the hazard lights or a warning sign behind the vehicle. 2. Call the rental shop. Ask for an emergency number when you rent, and ask whether someone can come out. Have your location, the plate number and a short description of the problem ready. 3. Find a repair shop. Small roadside motorbike workshops, signposted "sửa xe", are common along Vietnamese roads. Ask the price first and clear any bigger repair with the rental shop. 4. Walk it. Short distances can be done on foot, keeping to the right-hand edge and out of pedestrians' way. 5. Arrange transport. For longer distances, the shop or a workshop can often collect the bike with a pickup or small truck.

Our guide on what to do if your rental bike breaks down goes into more detail on handling a fault and what to agree with the shop.

Won't start? Check the simple things first

Not every bike that refuses to start is broken. Before calling for help, run a quick check. On many automatic scooters a brake lever has to be pulled to start, and some models will not start while the side stand is down. Also check whether the engine kill switch on the handlebar is flipped, whether there is fuel in the tank and whether the lights come on when you turn the ignition; if everything stays dark, the battery may be flat. Ask the shop at pickup how starting works on your model, and you will save time if it ever matters. Our guide to Honda Wave, Vision, Air Blade and Lead scooters describes the common rental models.

Breakdowns in tunnels and at night

Stricter duties apply if the bike dies inside a road tunnel. Under Article 26(2) of the law, the vehicle must be moved into an emergency bay; if that is impossible, switch on the hazard lights, place a warning sign at a safe distance behind the vehicle and inform the traffic police or the tunnel operator. At night, stay beside the roadway where you can be seen, and keep the bike's lights on as long as the battery allows. Our guide to riding at night covers visibility in the dark.

Empty tank: prevent rather than push

The most common reason a bike stops is simply an empty tank. In rural areas, fill up early, as soon as the gauge drops below half, because filling stations can be many kilometres apart on mountain routes. Check before a trip that the rental bike's fuel gauge works, and note the odometer reading after filling up so you learn how far one tank takes you.

Passengers and loads

The same rules apply without a breakdown. A passenger who carries bulky objects or holds on to another vehicle breaks Article 33(4); that includes a passenger who steers a second bike alongside by hand or grips another rider's bag to keep the two together. Carrying a second motorbike on your own is out of the question too. Our guide on how many people can ride one motorbike explains the passenger and luggage limits.

Frequently asked questions

What if I am somewhere remote and no help comes? Stay with the bike in a safe place and call the shop. A few hours' wait is better than a crash while towing. In an emergency, the police can be reached on 113 (THƯ VIỆN PHÁP LUẬT).

May someone else ride the bike to the workshop for me? Yes, if the bike is rideable and that person holds a suitable licence. Towing or pushing with a second bike remains banned either way.

In short

Towing a motorbike with another, or pushing it along with your foot from a moving bike, is banned by Article 33 and costs 600,000 to 800,000 VND under Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, or up to 14,000,000 VND if it causes an accident.

Instead, walk the bike to a safe spot, warn other traffic and call the shop that rented it to you or a workshop.
