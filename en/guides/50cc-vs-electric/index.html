50cc Moped or Electric Scooter in Hanoi?

Compare 50cc mopeds and electric scooters in Hanoi, including licence rules, battery planning, registration and how to choose for short city trips.

Two categories of rental two-wheelers in Hanoi sit just below the notice of most travellers, yet they solve the same problem in opposite ways: the 50cc moped and the electric scooter. Both promise maximum simplicity, and both are attractive for the same reason – under Vietnamese rules, each can be ridden without a motorcycle licence under specific conditions, because the law treats such machines as "xe gắn máy" rather than motorcycles (the Ministry of Public Security explains the category in its guidance on under-50cc vehicles). One escapes the licence requirement through engine size, the other through a speed- and power-limited electric design. Choosing between them is really a choice between two different daily routines: fuel stops versus charging plans, familiar mechanics versus near-silence. This guide compares both options for the specific conditions of Hanoi traffic. For everything that applies regardless of engine type, the rental checklist for Hanoi covers the handover.

The licence exemption: how both categories sidestep A1

Vietnam's Law on Order and Road Traffic Safety, 36/2024/QH15, has applied since 1 January 2025. The A1 class covers motorcycles up to 125cc or electric motors up to 11 kW; anything stronger falls under class A (law text: Luật Trật tự, an toàn giao thông đường bộ 36/2024/QH15; Danang Visa Run, driving licence guide 2026). Small vehicles sit outside this system: under Article 34 of the law a "xe gắn máy" is a two- or three-wheeler with a designed top speed of no more than 50 km/h and, for petrol engines, no more than 50 cm3; riders aged 16 and over may operate one without a driving licence, but must carry the vehicle's registration certificate and compulsory insurance (Ministry of Public Security; Osakar, 50cc licence rules). Electric two-wheelers follow the same definition: an electric model counts as licence-exempt only if its designed top speed does not exceed 50 km/h and its motor does not exceed 4 kW; faster or more powerful models return to A1 (Ministry of Public Security; Xe Điện Xanh Sài Gòn, electric licence rules).

For visitors, this symmetry matters more than it first appears. A foreign tourist cannot obtain a Vietnamese motorcycle licence at all on a tourist visa (Vietnam Easy Rider, licence rules for foreigners). An international driving permit is only useful if it was issued under the 1968 Vienna Convention and includes a motorcycle category: the UK Foreign Office advises British riders to carry a 1968 permit together with their UK licence (GOV.UK), while the U.S. State Department states plainly that U.S. driver's licences and international permits are not valid for driving in Vietnam (travel.state.gov). Canada and Australia are in the same position because they issue permits under the 1949 Geneva Convention, which Vietnam does not recognize (Ha Giang Trips). For riders from those countries, a licence-exempt 50cc moped or a low-speed electric scooter is not a budget fallback; it is the only category that offers a clean legal basis for riding at all. If your licence situation is solid, the choice becomes one of pure convenience.

The 50cc moped: familiar, fuel-driven, and scarcer than you think

Genuine 50cc models are rarer in Hanoi rental fleets than the category's reputation suggests. Because proper Honda 50cc machines carry a premium price on the Vietnamese market, shops keep only small contingents – often SYM or KYMCO units – and release them on request rather than from the front row (Hanoi Motorbike Rental). If a 50cc is the plan, request it in advance instead of expecting to find one on arrival.

The strengths are the ones riders know from home. Refuelling takes minutes at any of the thousands of street-side petrol stations, there is no charging infrastructure to think about, and the mechanical feel of a small petrol engine is familiar. The weaknesses are equally predictable: limited reserves for hills and a passenger, no headroom for long intercity distances, and a comfortable ceiling that ends where the city ends. In the Old Quarter, where traffic rarely exceeds 30 km/h, the small engine is barely a limitation; on bridge ramps and in headwinds you feel it immediately.

The electric scooter: charging routine, battery swap, and real range

Electric scooters in Hanoi are in the middle of a structural shift. VinFast, the country's largest manufacturer, is building out a battery-swap network with plans for up to 150,000 swap points. Its swappable models carry two 1.5 kWh battery packs, each good for roughly 85 km under ideal conditions; battery rental runs around 200,000 VND per month and a station swap costs about 9,000 VND (Vietnam News, battery swap report).

For a renter, this translates into three practical questions before signing anything. Does the scooter use swappable batteries or a fixed pack? Who pays for electricity, battery rental or swap fees during the rental period? And how far does the battery actually last on a Hanoi day? The third question deserves the most scepticism: quoted ranges assume ideal conditions, and stop-and-go traffic with a passenger cuts them noticeably. An electric scooter without a workable charging plan is not a convenience upgrade; it is a schedule risk.

One caution that experienced riders mention most: electric scooters are quiet. In Hanoi traffic, engine noise functions as a presence signal – pedestrians and car drivers register a petrol bike before they see it. On a near-silent machine you lose that channel, so compensate deliberately: ride with lights on, make eye contact at intersections, and keep generous following distances.

Papers, plates, and insurance: the obligations that remain

"No licence needed" is not "no paperwork needed". The vehicle itself must be registered and carry a plate, and in Vietnam electric machines are treated like motorized vehicles for this purpose; riding without a plate or registration papers risks fines under the schedule in Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP and temporary impoundment (Xe Xanh Viet, electric registration rules). At handover, check the same documents you would check on any scooter: registration papers, proof of insurance, and a clear agreement about damage responsibility. The rental checklist walks through these items in order.

Insurance deserves a separate note, because travellers routinely get this wrong. A travel insurance policy that covers motorcycle riding can still require that the rider holds the licences the law demands, and the UK Foreign Office warns that riding without the correct licence can leave you uninsured (GOV.UK). If your legal basis for riding is the under-50cc exemption, read the policy wording carefully before assuming coverage. This check belongs before booking, not at the pickup point.

Which option fits which trip

The 50cc moped fits if you: ride only within the city and its immediate surroundings, prefer refuelling over charging planning, need the bike for a few days, and keep a modest radius. The electric scooter fits if your daily distances are short and predictable, your hotel offers a socket or the shop provides swap batteries, and you can fold charging time into your day – over a meal, during work hours, overnight.

For riders with a recognized 1968 international permit that includes a motorcycle category, neither option is a legal necessity, and the choice is pure comfort. For riders from 1949-convention countries, this category is the whole legal playing field, and the decision is between two routines rather than between convenience and risk.

Three questions to ask the rental shop

First: is the machine a genuine 50cc model or a swap-battery electric, and are its papers complete? Second: what is the realistic range with two people in stop-and-go traffic? Third: who covers electricity, battery rental and swap fees during the rental? The answers determine the rhythm of your entire trip. The rest of the handover – brakes, tyres, lights, and photos of existing damage – is covered by the checklist, and the wider topic starts on the English homepage. Between 50 cubic centimetres and kilowatt-hours there is no objective winner, only the version that matches how you actually ride.
