Hanoi Traffic for First-Time Riders: How the Flow Works

How Hanoi motorbike traffic works for first-time riders: steady speeds, the left-turn crawl, one-way streets, roads closed to motorbikes, Decree 168/2024 fines police enforce, and a first-day plan.

The first thing a newcomer notices at a Hanoi intersection is that nobody seems to stop, and the second thing is that nobody seems to crash. Both observations are half right. Hanoi traffic is not lawless; it runs on a different set of expectations from the ones you learned at home, and once you can read them the city becomes rideable. This guide explains how the flow actually works, which of its habits are legal and which are merely common, and how to spend your first day on a rental bike without learning the hard way. It is written for someone who already knows how to ride and has sorted out the licence question; the handover checks that apply to any rental are in the pickup checklist.

Why the flow looks chaotic and is not

Hanoi moves on two wheels. The city counts more than eight million private vehicles, of which roughly 6.9 million are motorbikes and 1.1 million are cars, according to figures published by the city and reported by Dân trí. With that many small vehicles on narrow streets, the system cannot work on strict right of way; it works on predictability. Riders keep a steady speed, move in smooth lines and expect everyone else to do the same. The horn is not an insult; a short beep means "I am here, I am not stopping", and it is used so often that nobody reacts to it emotionally.

The speeds are low. On urban roads a motorbike may ride at most 60 km/h where the carriageway has a central divider and 50 km/h where it does not, and small mopeds are limited to 40 km/h, as the speed table from Circular 38/2024 explained by THƯ VIỆN PHÁP LUẬT shows. In practice the river of bikes in the centre runs at 20 to 30 km/h, which is what makes the constant merging survivable: at that speed people have time to adjust around you, provided you do not surprise them.

The three rules that actually govern the stream

  • Never stop suddenly, never swerve. The rider behind you is reading your line, not your brake light. If you need to slow, do it gradually; if you need to change direction, drift across in a smooth diagonal and let the people around you flow past.
  • Look ahead, not back. Vietnamese riders take responsibility for what is in front of them. If you are behind someone, their safety is your job; if you are in front, you are not expected to check mirrors for every bike closing in. Shoulder checks before a lane change are still wise, but the mental model is "protect the space ahead".
  • Pedestrians and bikes negotiate, they do not wait. Someone crossing the road walks at a constant pace and expects you to go around them. Do that; do not stop dead in the stream for a pedestrian who is clearly managing the crossing.

Intersections, left turns and roundabouts

At a signalled junction the light is obeyed more than it looks, and the fine for ignoring it is serious: not complying with a traffic light costs a motorbike rider 4,000,000 to 6,000,000 VND under Article 7 of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, and Hanoi has installed cameras at many central junctions. Turning right on red is only allowed where a sign or a separate green arrow permits it; plenty of locals do it anyway, which is not a reason for you to.

The Hanoi left turn is the manoeuvre that unnerves first-time riders. Instead of waiting for a gap, riders turning left across oncoming traffic edge forward slowly, let the oncoming bikes split around them and complete the turn as the stream thins. It works because everyone moves slowly and nobody stops. Your job is to be predictable: edge, hold a steady crawl, keep your eyes on the riders coming at you, and finish the turn when the gap opens. At roundabouts the same logic applies; traffic already in the circle does not have the right of way in practice, so enter slowly and merge rather than wait.

Lanes, buses, one-way streets and the roads you may not use

Motorbikes keep to the right-hand side of the carriageway and leave the left lanes to cars, which is both the rule and the safest place to be. City buses pull into stops without much warning; give them room on the right and never try to pass a bus on its kerb side. In the Old Quarter many streets are one-way and the direction can change at the next block. Riding against a one-way street or into a no-entry road is fined at 4,000,000 to 6,000,000 VND, and so is riding on the pavement to escape a jam, under the same Article 7 of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP.

Some roads are simply closed to motorbikes. The elevated section of Ring Road 2 is reserved for cars, with motorbikes, three-wheelers and non-motorised vehicles banned, as VOV reported when it opened; the same applies to the elevated Ring Road 3 and to expressway sections marked with a no-motorbike sign. Entering a road that is closed to your vehicle type costs 2,000,000 to 3,000,000 VND under Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, and navigation apps do not always keep you off them, so read the blue and red signs at every on-ramp.

The rules police really enforce on riders

Traffic police in Hanoi stop motorbikes for a short list of things, and almost all of them are easy to avoid. The figures below are from Article 7 and Article 18 of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP:

  • No helmet, or a helmet with the strap undone: 400,000 to 600,000 VND (Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP).
  • No lights between 18:00 and 06:00 or in rain, fog or dust: 200,000 to 400,000 VND (Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP).
  • Holding and using a phone while riding: 800,000 to 1,000,000 VND. Mount it or stop (Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP).
  • A second adult passenger: 400,000 to 600,000 VND; a motorbike carries one passenger, with exceptions for a child under 12, a patient being taken to hospital or an elderly or disabled person (Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP).
  • Riding without a licence that matches the bike: 2,000,000 to 4,000,000 VND up to 125 cc and 6,000,000 to 8,000,000 VND above that (Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP).

Alcohol is the one with no tolerance at all. Any measurable alcohol is an offence for a rider, with fines rising through three tiers to 8,000,000 to 10,000,000 VND and a long licence suspension at the top, as the Cần Thơ Department of Justice sets out in its summary of the 2025 alcohol fines. Hanoi's bia hơi corners and its rental scooters do not mix; walk or take a taxi back.

The rhythm of a Hanoi day

Traffic peaks from roughly seven to nine in the morning and five to seven in the evening, when ring roads and the bridges across the Red River crawl. Midday is easier, and early morning around West Lake or the Ba Đình boulevards is the best time to learn. Rain changes everything: the traffic police advise slowing to no more than 40 km/h and switching on low beams in bad weather, as VnExpress reported, and the painted lines and metal covers stay slippery long after a shower. Night riding is where visitors most often come to grief: the UK Foreign Office's travel advice for Vietnam is blunt about the seriousness of road accidents for foreign visitors, and the combination of unlit bikes, unexpected pedestrians and late-evening drinking makes the hours after dark the ones to be most careful in.

If something goes wrong

Stop, get the bike and yourself off the carriageway, and only then start phoning. If anyone is hurt, call 115 for an ambulance and 113 for the police; THƯ VIỆN PHÁP LUẬT explains what each Vietnamese emergency number does. Photograph everything before anything is moved, call your rental shop before agreeing to anything with the other party, and do not hand over your passport to settle a dispute on the spot. The compulsory insurance on the bike covers third parties only, up to the fixed limits in Decree 67/2023/NĐ-CP, so your own travel insurance and your licence status decide what the accident costs you.

A first-day plan that works

Collect the bike in the morning, not at rush hour. Ride for twenty minutes on quiet streets to learn its brakes and its horn. Do one simple loop with a single left turn and one roundabout, then stop for a coffee and think about what you saw. Only after that head for the centre, and keep the first trips to places you can reach without the Old Quarter's one-way maze. The English pillar page links to the guides on licences, deposits and rainy-season riding that fill in the rest; this page only covers the flow, and the flow is learnable in a day if you give it one.
