What to Wear Riding a Motorbike in Vietnam

What to wear on a rented motorbike in Vietnam: helmet rules and fines, clothing for heat and sun, shoes, gloves, eye protection, rain gear, layers for the cool north and visibility at night.

Many visitors ride their first scooter in Vietnam in shorts, a T-shirt and flip-flops, because that is what the heat suggests and what they see around them. Clothing cannot prevent a crash, but it decides how badly a small slide on the asphalt ends, how long you can ride in the midday sun and whether other road users see you after dark. This guide starts with the one item the law requires, the helmet, and then goes through clothing for heat, rain and the cool northern season, footwear, gloves, eye protection and visibility. For the wider picture on safe riding, see our topic page on safer riding and breakdown support.

Start with the helmet

Under Article 33(2) of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety, the rider and every passenger on a motorbike or moped must wear a helmet that meets the national technical standard, with the chin strap fastened correctly. Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP fines a rider without a helmet or with an unfastened strap 400,000 to 600,000 VND under Article 7(2)(h), and the same amount applies under Article 7(2)(i) when a passenger rides without a proper helmet, apart from a few exceptions such as children under 6.

The law sets the minimum; fit and quality are up to you. The French foreign ministry advises travellers to bring a helmet that meets European standards, and the UK travel advice reminds visitors that riding without a helmet is against the law. If you use the rental shop's helmet, try several: it should sit level, not rock when you shake your head, and the strap should close under the chin with no more than two fingers of space. A visor or a pair of glasses protects your eyes from dust, insects and rain.

Clothing for heat and sun

Vietnam's summer is hot, and on a motorbike you sit in the sun for hours. The WHO heat and health fact sheet recommends light, loose-fitting clothing, staying in the shade and drinking water regularly, and it points out that the perceived temperature in direct sun can be 10 to 15 degrees higher than in the shade. Long, thin sleeves and trousers in light colours protect against sunburn and also give some protection from scrapes in a minor fall, which shorts and sleeveless tops do not.

Plan your day around the heat as well: ride early in the morning and late in the afternoon, and take long breaks in the shade at midday. A thin scarf or buff protects the neck, and sunscreen on the backs of the hands and on the neck makes a long ride far more comfortable.

Shoes, not flip-flops

Closed shoes are the most underrated piece of gear. Your feet are the first thing that touches the road when you stop on gravel or put a foot down in a puddle, and the exhaust pipe on many scooters sits exactly where a passenger's calf or ankle rests when getting off. Trainers or light boots that cover the ankle protect against burns, stubbed toes and slips. If you only packed sandals, at least choose a pair with a strap at the heel so that it cannot slip off while you ride.

Gloves and eye protection

Light gloves protect your hands from sunburn and from grazes if you fall, and they give a better grip when the handlebars are wet. Motorbike gloves are sold in many shops in Hanoi, but simple cycling gloves already help. For your eyes, a clear visor or clear glasses are the best choice; a tinted visor is pleasant by day but dangerous at dusk and at night, as our guide to riding a motorbike at night in Vietnam explains.

Rain gear

Heavy showers can arrive within minutes in the rainy season. Many riders use a poncho, which is cheap and quick to put on, but it can flap in the wind, block your view of the indicators and catch on the wheel if it hangs too low. A two-piece rain suit, jacket and trousers, is safer for longer rides. Whatever you choose, make sure it does not cover the lights or the number plate. Our guide to riding a scooter in Hanoi during heavy rain covers braking, flooded streets and visibility in more detail.

Layers for the cool north

From late autumn to early spring, northern Vietnam can feel cold on a motorbike, especially in the mountains and in drizzle. The wind chill on a moving bike makes a mild day feel much colder. Bring layers: a thin base layer, a fleece or light down jacket, and a windproof outer layer, plus a scarf and full-finger gloves. Keep your rain gear on top when the drizzle sets in, because wet clothing loses its warmth quickly.

Be seen

Dark clothing disappears in traffic at dusk. Bright colours and a light reflective vest make you easier to see, and a vest weighs almost nothing in a daypack. Under Article 20(1) of the Law, the headlight must be on between 18:00 and 06:00 and in fog, rain or other poor visibility, and your clothing should work with it, not against it.

Passengers need the same care

A passenger is exposed to the same sun, rain and road as the rider, and the helmet rule in Article 33(2) applies to them too. Passengers also have their own risks: the exhaust pipe sits next to the passenger footrest on most scooters, so long trousers and closed shoes matter even more for the person on the back. Ask passengers to keep their feet on the pegs, to hold on to the rider or the grab rail rather than to bags, and not to let scarves, straps or long rain ponchos hang down near the rear wheel.

A quick checklist before you ride

  • Helmet that fits, strap fastened, clear visor or glasses.
  • Long, light clothing for sun, or layers and a windproof jacket for the cool season.
  • Closed shoes that cover the ankle.
  • Light gloves.
  • Rain suit or poncho that does not cover lights or plate.
  • Something bright or reflective for the evening.
  • Water and sunscreen in the under-seat compartment.

Check the bike itself just as carefully when you pick it up; our pickup checklist lists what to look at.

In short

The helmet is the only item the law requires, and riding without it or with the strap open costs 400,000 to 600,000 VND under Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP. Everything else is about comfort and limiting harm: light, long clothing against sun and scrapes, as the WHO recommends for heat, closed shoes, gloves, clear eye protection, proper rain gear, warm layers in the north and something bright for the evening.
