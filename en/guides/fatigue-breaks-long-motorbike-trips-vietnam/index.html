Fatigue on Long Motorbike Trips in Vietnam

How to avoid fatigue on multi-day motorbike trips in Vietnam: realistic day stages, a break at least every two hours, no night riding, warning signs and what the law says about speed.

A motorbike trip through Vietnam tempts you into long days. The scenery is spectacular, the next stop always seems close, and 250 kilometres looks easy on the map. On a scooter, though, a lot adds up: heat or cold, noise, vibration, traffic that needs your full attention and mountain roads where you are constantly steering and braking. Fatigue creeps up gradually, and it slows your reactions exactly when they matter. This guide shows how to plan day stages and breaks so that you arrive rested. For an overview of riding safely, see our topic page on safer riding and breakdown support.

What the Highway Code recommends

Vietnamese law has no rule on breaks for private riders. A good benchmark is the UK's Highway Code, whose rule 91 states that driving when tired greatly increases the risk of a collision and recommends (GOV.UK, Highway Code, rule 91):

  • Start rested: do not begin a journey if you feel tired, and get a good night's sleep before a long one.
  • Avoid the small hours: avoid long journeys between midnight and 6 am, when natural alertness is at its lowest.
  • Plan breaks: a break of at least 15 minutes after every two hours of driving is recommended.
  • Stop when sleepy: if you feel sleepy, stop in a safe place.

The rule is written for drivers and motorcyclists in the UK; for scooter trips in Vietnam it makes a sensible minimum, because on two wheels you are far more exposed to the weather than in a car.

What Vietnamese law requires

Even without a rule on breaks, fatigue has legal consequences. Article 12(2) of Law 36/2024/QH15 requires every rider to keep to a speed that suits the condition of bridges and roads, the traffic density, the terrain, the weather and other factors, to keep the road safe. A rider who is exhausted and reacts late can hardly meet that duty. A rider who causes an accident by failing to watch, slow down or stop as required faces 10,000,000 to 14,000,000 VND under Article 7(10)(a) of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, and loses 10 points.

Realistic day stages

How far you get in a day depends less on the kilometres than on the road. On good national highways you make steady progress; on mountain roads with hairpins, roadworks and village centres the same distance can take several times as long. So plan in hours, not kilometres:

1. Cap your riding time. Work out the day's actual riding time and allow a buffer for breaks, photo stops, fuel and delays. 2. Arrive before dark. Plan the stage so you reach your destination well before sunset. France's foreign ministry expressly advises travellers to avoid driving at night (France Diplomatie); our guide to riding at night explains why. 3. Build in rest days. After two or three long days, a day off the bike does more than any break. 4. Ride the hard parts early. Tackle the most demanding section, such as a pass, in the morning while you are freshest.

A sample day in the mountains

A mountain stage that follows the Highway Code's advice on breaks might look like this. Set off shortly after sunrise and take a first break after just under two hours at a viewpoint or café, then ride the hardest section of the day while you are still fresh. Around midday, take a long break with food, water and shade. In the afternoon, ride no more than another two hours with a short stop in between, and arrive well before dark. If the plan is not working out, shorten the stage rather than riding on into dusk.

A good riding position saves energy

Good posture tires you less. The Oregon motorcycle manual advises sitting with your back straight, keeping your head and eyes up and sitting so that your arms are free to steer rather than holding up your upper body, with your feet on the footrests while riding (Oregon DMV, Section 4). Hanging off the bars with stiff arms leaves you more exhausted after an hour than you need to be.

Breaks that actually refresh you

A break only refreshes you if you really get off the bike. Take your helmet off, drink water, eat something and move around for a few minutes. In hot weather, take the break in the shade or somewhere air-conditioned; our guide to riding in extreme heat explains how much to drink.

Warning signs of fatigue

Fatigue announces itself, even if you do not want to admit it. Typical signs are frequent yawning, heavy eyelids, trouble holding your line, missed turnings or signs, and not remembering the last few kilometres. At that point at the latest, the Highway Code's rule applies: stop somewhere safe (GOV.UK, rule 91). Do not push on "just to get there".

Riding in a group

In a group, the pressure to keep up is especially strong. Agree on fixed breaks, for example every two hours, and let the slowest or most tired rider set the pace. Anyone who is tired says so, and the group stops. Our guide to riding motorbikes in a group covers the rest of group riding.

Alcohol, medication and lack of sleep

Other factors make fatigue worse. Vietnam has a zero-alcohol rule for riders (Article 9(2) of the law), and some medicines cause drowsiness. If you plan a late night and an early start, push the start back. Our guides to the zero-alcohol rule and to drugs, medication and riding explain the rules.

Frequently asked questions

How many kilometres can you ride in a day? There is no fixed figure. In two hours you cover far more ground on a good road than on a mountain pass with roadworks. Plan by riding hours and breaks, and check the route in a map app beforehand.

What if I get tired but cannot find a good place to stop? Ride on slowly to the next safe spot, such as a fuel station, a café or a wide verge away from bends. Do not stop on a blind bend; Article 18(4)(b) of the law forbids it.

In short

Start rested, avoid riding between midnight and 6 am, take at least 15 minutes' break after two hours at the latest and stop as soon as you feel tired (GOV.UK, Highway Code, rule 91). Plan stages in hours rather than kilometres and arrive before dark. Your speed must always suit the road, the traffic and the weather (Article 12(2)).
