Refuelling a Rental Motorbike in Vietnam: RON 92 or RON 95?

Renting a motorbike in Hanoi? Learn the difference between E5 RON 92 and RON 95 (E10 since 1 June 2026), which fuel your rental bike actually needs and how a fuel stop at a Vietnamese station works.

You have rented a motorbike in Hanoi, done a few loops around the Old Quarter, and the fuel gauge is already heading down. At the station you face two names you may never have seen before: E5 RON 92 and RON 95. Which one is right for your rental bike? The short answer: most scooters and manual motorcycles rented in Vietnam are designed for RON 92, and today's RON 95 is safe for those machines too, because manufacturers have been designing around ethanol-blended fuel for years. This guide explains what changed at Vietnamese fuel stations on 1 June 2026, how to check on the bike itself which fuel the manufacturer recommends, and how a fuel stop works in practice. It belongs to our overview of rental costs, deposits and fees in Hanoi.

What you actually get at a Vietnamese fuel station

Vietnam has two ordinary petrol grades for motorbikes. The first is E5 RON 92: petrol with an octane rating of 92, blended with five percent bioethanol. The second is RON 95, traditionally the higher-octane grade. Since 1 June 2026, that second grade has been E10 across the country under the official fuel regulation: petrol blended with ten percent bioethanol, based on Circular 50/2025/TT-BCT of the Ministry of Industry and Trade (explanation of the RON95/RON92 switchover, thuvienphapluat.vn). E5 RON 92 remains available, under the same regulation, until 31 December 2030. For you as a renter this means one practical thing: whichever pump you choose, you are always filling up an ethanol blend. Pure fossil petrol is no longer sold on the regular network, and that is exactly the reality manufacturers have designed their engines around for years.

Ethanol in petrol is neither new nor exotic. It raises the blend's knock resistance and lowers certain emissions, but it holds slightly less energy per litre than fossil petrol and can absorb moisture from the air. At percentages up to ten this is no concern for a modern engine; above that threshold, real risks appear for rubber hoses and the fuel system. That is why the ten percent limit is written into Vietnam's national product standard, and the whole market complies with it.

What fuel does your rental bike need?

Most scooters rented in Hanoi are Honda or Yamaha models with 110 to 160 cc engines and low compression: Honda Vision, Honda Lead, Honda Air Blade, Yamaha Grande, or the classic foot-shifted Honda Wave. For all of them the manufacturer specifies petrol with an octane rating around 92. You do not have to guess: nearly every bike carries a sticker at or beside the filler cap stating the recommended octane, and the owner's manual repeats the same figure. If you are unsure, ask at handover what fuel the rental shop itself uses; a good rental operator answers without hesitation.

Important for anyone worried about the new E10: Honda Vietnam confirmed in an official note from August 2025 that the brand's vehicles run without problems on petrol with an octane rating of at least 91 and an ethanol content of up to ten percent, exactly as the Vietnamese standard QCVN 01:2022/BKHCN prescribes (Honda Vietnam on E10 petrol). Both E5 RON 92 and E10 RON 95 therefore sit inside what the engine tolerates. There is no reason to rewrite a planned route just because a station sells only RON 95.

RON 92 versus RON 95 in practice

The octane number says nothing about the "quality" of the petrol; it measures resistance to self-ignition under pressure. High-compression engines, usually sportier models, need a higher rating so the mixture does not ignite early. An ordinary rental scooter does not have that high compression: for these bikes RON 92 is exactly right, and RON 95 delivers no extra power, no cleaner combustion and no longer engine life. The difference is mostly in your wallet, because RON 95 costs more at the pump. Both prices are regulated by the Vietnamese state and adjusted at intervals, which is why this guide quotes no current figures.

For a fair comparison: because it contains ethanol, E10 holds slightly less energy per litre than pure petrol, which shows up as a marginally higher consumption. In everyday city use on a rental scooter the difference is too small to notice. In short: if your bike is built for 92, E5 RON 92 is the cheapest and entirely correct choice; where RON 92 is unavailable, E10 RON 95 is a safe alternative with no risk to the engine.

How a fuel stop works at a Vietnamese station

Vietnamese fuel stations are usually small and busy, and staff almost always fill up for you. You park at the right pump, take off your helmet, say what you want and wait for the nozzle to click off. Two phrases do wonders: "đầy bình" means "fill it up"; if you want a fixed amount, name the sum and the attendant pumps exactly that much. Small notes are handy, though many city stations also accept a QR code for payment by phone. Self-service is usually unnecessary, and often not possible at all.

A few habits save trouble: switch the engine off before filling, screw the cap tight before you ride off, do not smoke or bring a flame near, and keep the filler-cap key handy if your bike has a separate lock. A scooter tank takes only a few litres; the whole stop lasts two or three minutes.

Choose an official station of a known brand. Petrolimex, the country's largest fuel chain, lists E10 RON95-V, E10 RON95-III and E5 RON92-II among its products (petrolimex.com.vn). On rural roads you will sometimes meet informal sellers with petrol in plastic bottles. The quality and origin of that fuel cannot be verified and the blend ratio is rarely right; do not fill up there, even when you are in a hurry. Within Hanoi itself, official stations sit close enough together that you can always fuel up within a few kilometres.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The three classic mistakes when fuelling a rental bike are quickly listed. The first is panic after putting RON 95 into a bike built for 92: that is completely harmless, because a higher octane rating is not a bad fuel, only a more expensive one. The second is the opposite fear, that ethanol "ruins" the engine; within the ten percent the standard allows and the manufacturer designs for, that does not happen. The third, and the only genuinely risky one, is riding everywhere on reserve and accepting whatever pump appears first.

If something feels wrong after a fill-up – stuttering, power loss, an engine that cuts out – do not abandon the bike at the roadside. Park somewhere safe, call the rental shop and follow the steps in our guide to breakdowns with a rental bike. Often the cause is not the fuel at all but something as simple as a wet spark plug after a downpour.

Fuel, budget and returning the bike

Budget for fuel alongside the rent and the deposit; it is one of the fixed posts of any rental period. Because prices are state-regulated and change periodically, the best move is to ask the rental shop roughly what a full tank costs for your model, and set that against the costs beyond the rental fee. Anyone renting for more than a week can compare terms in our guide to daily, weekly and monthly rentals.

Photograph the fuel level at handover, because many rental shops expect the same level when you return the bike. Check what the contract says about fuel, too: some shops charge a fee if the bike comes back with the reserve light on. More on contracts and deposits is in our explanation of rental deposits. Finally, if you plan to ride in central Hanoi, read up on the low-emission zone inside Ring Road 1 first, because that rule targets exactly the petrol-powered scooters and light motorbikes.
