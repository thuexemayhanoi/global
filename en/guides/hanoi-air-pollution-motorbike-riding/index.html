Hanoi Air Pollution and Motorbike Riding

How to read Hanoi's official air quality index before a motorbike ride, what the six AQI bands mean, what the WHO says about PM2.5 and how riders can cut their exposure on bad-air days.

On a motorbike in Hanoi you breathe the street's air without a filter: exhaust, dust from building sites and, on some days, a visible haze over the city. Visitors see high AQI numbers in news reports and apps and wonder what that means for their plans. This guide shows where to find the city's official air quality readings, how to read the index and the health advice beneath it, what the World Health Organization says about particle pollution and how to reduce your exposure as a rider. For an overview of riding safely, see our topic page on safer riding and breakdown support.

Where to check official readings

The city of Hanoi runs an environmental monitoring portal on which its monitoring stations show the current air quality index (Hanoi environmental monitoring portal). For each station you see the value, a category and short health advice for the general public and for sensitive groups. The index is calculated under a technical guideline of Vietnam's environment administration, Decision 1459/QĐ-TCMT of 2019 (Hanoi environmental monitoring portal). Readings vary a lot by place and time of day, so look at the station closest to your route rather than a city-wide figure.

The six AQI bands

The portal sorts values into six bands (Hanoi environmental monitoring portal):

| AQI | Category | What the portal says | |---|---|---| | 0–50 | Good | Air quality is good and has no health effects. | | 51–100 | Moderate | Air quality is acceptable, but sensitive people may notice some effects. | | 101–150 | Poor | Sensitive people develop health problems; effects on everyone else are slight. | | 151–200 | Unhealthy | Healthy people feel effects too; sensitive groups may have more serious problems. | | 201–300 | Very unhealthy | Health warning: everyone is more seriously affected. | | Above 300 | Hazardous | Health emergency: the whole population is seriously affected. |

The portal counts children, older people and people with respiratory illness among the sensitive groups (Hanoi environmental monitoring portal).

Read the advice, not just the number

Under each value the portal gives separate advice for people without health conditions and for sensitive groups. When we checked on 15 October 2026 at 9 a.m., it showed an AQI of 85, in the moderate band. Healthy people could be outdoors without restriction, the advice said, while sensitive people should watch for symptoms such as coughing or shortness of breath but could stay active outside (Hanoi environmental monitoring portal). The advice changes with the value, so read the text as well as the figure, especially if you travel with children, older relatives or a respiratory condition.

Why one number is not the whole story

The portal's map shows several monitoring points at once. In the same hour, the points on the map ranged from 62 to 126, from moderate to poor (Hanoi environmental monitoring portal). A single value says little about what you breathe at a particular junction. For planning, check the point nearest your route, and on a longer ride check again during the day.

Alongside the index, the portal lists limit values for the main pollutants, including sulphur dioxide, carbon monoxide, nitrogen dioxide, ozone, total dust, PM10 and PM2.5, based on the national technical regulation on air quality QCVN 05:2023/BTNMT (Hanoi environmental monitoring portal). For most visitors the AQI is enough, because it turns those measurements into one easy band.

What the WHO says about particle pollution

In its answers on air pollution in Vietnam, the WHO explains that polluted air raises the risk of respiratory infections, heart disease, stroke and lung cancer, and that both short and long exposures are linked to health effects (WHO Vietnam). The most harmful are particles smaller than 2.5 micrometres (PM2.5), because they reach deep into the lungs. For Hanoi, the same source gives an annual average of 47.9 micrograms of PM2.5 per cubic metre for 2016 (WHO Vietnam).

For acute episodes of high pollution, the WHO suggests staying indoors and reducing physical exertion for sensitive groups. It also notes that road traffic contributes between 15 and 70 percent of urban air pollution, depending on the city (WHO Vietnam).

A rider's plan for bad-air days

On a motorbike you sit right in the traffic, often in the exhaust of buses and lorries. Some practical conclusions:

  • Check before you ride. A look at the portal tells you whether today is a good or a bad day.
  • Shorten trips on bad days. At 151 or above, the unhealthy band, postpone longer rides or take a taxi for long distances.
  • Keep away from exhaust. At lights, avoid stopping right behind a bus or lorry tailpipe when you can.
  • Protect sensitive companions. Children, older people and anyone with asthma or heart disease should not spend hours on a bike on bad days.
  • Listen to your body. Coughing, shortness of breath or stinging eyes are reasons to stop and take a break indoors.

Whether a face mask makes sense for you, and which type, is best discussed with a doctor before the trip, especially if you have a health condition.

Traffic, the city and the low-emission zone

Riders are part of the traffic as well as its victims. A well-maintained bike, smooth riding without needless revving and walking short distances all help a little. The city is also acting on traffic: according to an announcement on the government portal, Hanoi's Department of Agriculture and Environment has published its project for a low-emission zone inside Ring Road 1. The announcement states that there has been no blanket ban on petrol and diesel vehicles inside Ring Road 1 since 1 July 2026. Instead, a pilot phase until the end of 2026 restricts vehicles by type and time in two areas of Hoàn Kiếm; in area 1, motorbikes, mopeds and cars may not enter on Fridays, Saturdays and Sundays from 7 p.m. to midnight (government portal). From 2028 the zone is to cover the whole of Ring Road 1, and motorbikes and mopeds that do not meet emission level 3 will then no longer be allowed in (government portal). Our guide to the Ring Road 1 low-emission zone explains what that means for renters.

Eyes and skin

Dust and exhaust irritate eyes and skin too. A helmet with a clear visor or a pair of glasses protects your eyes, and long sleeves protect your skin. Contact lens wearers should carry eye drops. After a long ride in the city, washing your face and hands feels good.

Frequently asked questions

Is Hanoi's air bad every day? No. Readings change by day, time and place; on the morning we checked, the value shown was in the moderate band (Hanoi environmental monitoring portal). Check the day you ride rather than relying on general statements.

What should I do if I feel unwell while riding? Stop somewhere safe, take a break indoors and only ride on once you feel better. If shortness of breath continues, get medical help.

In short

Hanoi's official readings are on the city's environmental monitoring portal; from an AQI of 101 sensitive people are affected, and from 151 everyone else too (Hanoi environmental monitoring portal). The WHO singles out PM2.5 as especially harmful and advises staying indoors and reducing exertion during high-pollution episodes (WHO Vietnam). Check the index before you ride, shorten trips on bad days and protect sensitive companions.
