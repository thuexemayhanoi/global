Animals on the Road in Vietnam

How to pass water buffalo, dogs and chickens on a motorbike in Vietnam, what the livestock warning sign W.230 means, what people leading animals must do and what the law asks of riders.

Ride out of Hanoi on a rented motorbike and the road soon stops belonging to vehicles alone. Dogs doze on the warm edge of the tarmac, chickens dash between houses, and across the rice plains and mountain valleys water buffalo, cows and goats wander over the road, sometimes with a herder, sometimes on their own. Animals are an underrated hazard for riders because they do not move like people or vehicles. This guide explains what the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety asks of riders and of people leading animals, what the warning signs mean and how to get past safely. For an overview of riding safely, see our topic page on safer riding and breakdown support.

What the law asks of you

Article 12(3) of Law 36/2024/QH15 lists the situations in which road users must watch, slow down or stop to keep the road safe. Point (g) names domestic animals ("vật nuôi") walking on the road or grazing beside it. The duty applies whatever the speed limit: even on a road where 60 km/h is allowed, you have to slow down as soon as a cow is grazing on the verge.

Ignoring that duty becomes expensive if it leads to a crash. Under Article 7(10)(a) of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, a motorbike rider who fails to watch, slow down or stop as required and causes an accident faces 10,000,000 to 14,000,000 VND, and Article 7(13)(d) takes 10 points off the licence (Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP).

The livestock warning sign

Where animals are regularly on the road, you will often pass a triangular warning sign. The national road sign standard QCVN 41:2024/BGTVT has two of them:

  • W.230 "Gia súc" (livestock): it warns of stretches where cattle roam freely or are driven across the road, for example near pastures and livestock farms. Under the standard, road users must ride slowly, keep watch and stop so the animals can cross safely.
  • W.231 "Thú rừng vượt qua đường" (wild animals crossing): it warns of stretches where wild animals cross, such as roads through forests or nature reserves. An additional plate S.501 may show how long the stretch is.

No sign does not mean no duty. Article 12(3) applies everywhere, and the sign is a reminder rather than a precondition. Our guide to Vietnam road signs explains the other signs you will see most often.

What people leading animals must do

People who lead or herd animals on the road have rules to follow too. Under Article 32 of the law they must:

  • keep the animals close to the right-hand edge of the road,
  • watch before taking animals across and cross only when it is safe,
  • not obstruct other road users,
  • not lead animals into lanes meant for motor vehicles, and
  • not leave animals loose on the road.

Under Article 11 of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, people in charge of animals who break these rules face 150,000 to 250,000 VND, for example when the animals endanger other road users or droppings are not cleared away, and 400,000 to 600,000 VND for leading animals onto the wrong part of the road or into areas where they are not allowed.

None of this gives you the right to force your way through. A buffalo has not read the decree, and being in the right is little comfort after a fall.

Never lead an animal from a moving bike

Now and then you will see someone walking a dog on a lead beside their moving motorbike. That is not allowed: under Article 7(3)(e) of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, leading an animal from a moving motorbike costs 600,000 to 800,000 VND.

Passing animals safely

Each kind of animal behaves differently. These rules of thumb help:

  • Water buffalo and cows: slow, heavy and quite happy to stand in the middle of the road. Pass at walking pace with as much room as you can, and never ride between a calf and its mother. If a herder is waiting at the edge with a herd, stop and let the animals cross first.
  • Dogs: they often bolt without warning or chase a passing bike. Stay calm, hold your line and do not kick out at the dog; that only unbalances you.
  • Chickens, ducks and geese: there is no predicting which way they will run. Roll off the throttle as soon as you see poultry pecking at the roadside, and expect them to go the wrong way.
  • Tethered animals: a cow or goat grazing on a rope tied to a stake can wander, and the rope may then stretch across the edge of the road. Keep away from the verge when you see tethered animals.
  • Goats on slopes: on mountain roads they like to jump from the bank onto the tarmac. Take blind bends especially slowly; our guide to narrow mountain roads explains who gives way where the road is tight.

Do not lean on the horn either. A startled animal often jumps exactly where you want to go. Our guide to horn rules explains when the horn is allowed.

At dusk and after dark

Around dusk, herds are often walked home from the fields, and dark animals are hard to see on unlit country roads. Your headlight must be on from 18:00 to 06:00 and whenever fog, rain or dust limits visibility (Article 20(1) of the law), but even a good headlight shows a black buffalo late. Ride much more slowly in the countryside after sunset and plan your days so that you arrive before dark. Our guide to riding at night has more on lights and visibility.

If you do hit an animal

Stop, make the scene safe and look after any injured people first. Tell the rental shop and, if anyone is hurt or the damage is significant, the police. If a dog bites or scratches you, get medical help the same day. Our guide on what to do after a motorbike accident explains the next steps, and our page on emergency numbers lists who to call.

Frequently asked questions

A herd is blocking the whole road. What should I do? Stop at a distance and wait until the animals have passed or the herder waves you on. Do not ride through the middle of the herd and do not rev the engine. Stopping is exactly what Article 12(3)(g) provides for in this situation.

Does the duty cover village dogs and cats as well? Yes. The law speaks of animals that people keep in general, not only of livestock. In villages and on market streets, dogs and poultry often roam unsupervised, and slow riding is called for there anyway.

Who pays if a loose cow damages my rental bike? That depends on the circumstances and on your rental contract, so talk to the shop straight away and photograph the scene. Our rental pickup checklist helps you record the bike's condition before you set off, which makes such conversations easier.

In short

Where animals walk on the road or graze beside it, you must slow down or stop under Article 12(3)(g), whether or not a W.230 sign is posted. People leading animals must keep them to the right-hand edge and must not leave them loose on the road (Article 32). Leading an animal from a moving motorbike costs 600,000 to 800,000 VND (Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP). Slowly, with plenty of room and without fuss, you will get past any herd.
