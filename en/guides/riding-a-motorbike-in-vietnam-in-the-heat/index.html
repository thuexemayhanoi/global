Riding a Motorbike in Vietnam in Extreme Heat

How to ride a rental motorbike safely through Vietnam's hottest days: when to ride, how much to drink, what to wear, who is most at risk and when to stop.

Vietnam's hot, dry months tempt many visitors into long motorbike trips: long days, little rain, open roads. Heat on a bike is easy to underestimate, though. You sit in the sun for hours, the airflow hides how hot it really is, and the helmet traps warmth around your head. This guide sums up what the World Health Organization advises about heat and health and turns it into a practical plan for hot days on a rental motorbike. For an overview of riding safely, see our topic page on safer riding and breakdown support.

Why heat and riding don't mix

The WHO describes heat as a major health hazard. Heat stress is one of the leading causes of weather-related death, it worsens existing conditions such as heart disease, diabetes and asthma, and it raises the risk of accidents; heatstroke is a medical emergency (WHO fact sheet on heat and health). On a motorbike those risks come together. Your body loses water, your concentration slips, and the traffic around you still demands full attention.

The airflow is the trap. While you are moving, the wind cools your skin and makes the heat feel bearable, even as your body keeps losing fluid. The first red light in full sun usually shows how hot it really is.

Plan the day around the heat

The WHO advises against going outside or doing strenuous activity during the hottest part of the day, recommends staying in the shade and spending two to three hours a day somewhere cool, and points out that in direct sun it can feel 10 to 15 degrees hotter (WHO). For a riding day that means:

  • Start early. Ride your longest stretch in the early morning, when the air is still fresh.
  • Stop for the middle of the day. Spend the midday hours somewhere shaded or air-conditioned: a café, a museum, lunch with a long break.
  • Finish in the late afternoon. Ride the rest of the way later, but time it so you arrive before dark. Our guide to riding at night explains why night riding is a risk of its own.
  • Watch the warnings. The WHO recommends following official heat warnings (WHO).

A sample day trip out of the city could look like this: leave soon after sunrise and ride the first leg in one go, take a drink break in the shade around half past nine, spend the middle of the day at your destination, perhaps over a long lunch or in an air-conditioned museum, and head back in the late afternoon. That keeps you off the bike during the hottest hours, as the WHO advises (WHO), and still gets you home before dark.

Drink on a schedule, not on thirst

The WHO recommends drinking water regularly, around one cup an hour and at least two to three litres a day (WHO). On a motorbike you hardly drink while moving, so build fixed drink stops into the plan, roughly once an hour, and keep a bottle of water in the storage space under the seat. That compartment heats up in the sun as well; an insulated bottle keeps water cool for longer.

Dress for the sun, not just the heat

The WHO advises light, loose-fitting clothing and cooling the skin with a damp cloth or damp, light clothing (WHO). On a motorbike, long, thin sleeves and trousers in light colours do two jobs: they prevent sunburn and give at least some protection against grazes in a fall. A thin scarf you wet at each stop cools your neck, and sunscreen on the backs of your hands and neck is easy to forget. Our guide to what to wear on a motorbike covers protective gear in more detail.

The helmet stays on

However hot it gets under the helmet, it is compulsory. Article 33(2) of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety requires rider and passengers to wear a helmet meeting the national standard, with the chin strap fastened. Riding without one, or with the strap open, costs 400,000 to 600,000 VND under Article 7(2)(h) of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP. A well-ventilated helmet helps against the heat; take it off at every stop to let your head cool down.

Who is most at risk

Children, older people and people with heart, lung or kidney disease are especially vulnerable to heat, according to the WHO (WHO). For children it recommends light, loose clothing that covers the skin, plus a sun hat, sunglasses and sunscreen, and it warns never to leave a child in a parked vehicle (WHO). If you are travelling with children or older companions, plan shorter legs, more breaks and an air-conditioned taxi for the hottest hours.

Warning signs: when to stop riding

Keep an eye on yourself and on anyone riding with you. If you feel dizzy, confused, sick or unusually exhausted, stop riding straight away, get into the shade, drink water and cool your skin. If things do not improve or get worse, call for medical help; heatstroke is an emergency (WHO). Do not ride on in that state, because a tired rider reacts too late. If something does happen on the road, our guide on what to do after a motorbike accident covers the first steps.

What to carry on a hot day

  • one large bottle of water, better two,
  • sunscreen and a thin cloth to wet,
  • sunglasses or a helmet with a visor,
  • a light long-sleeved shirt against the sun,
  • a power bank and the rental shop's phone number.

Your bike in the heat

The bike feels the heat too. At stops, park in the shade where you can so the seat and handlebars do not become too hot to touch, and check tyre pressure and oil before a longer trip. If something goes wrong, our guide on what to do if your rental bike breaks down explains the next steps.

Air-conditioned alternatives

Not every journey has to be done on two wheels at 35 degrees. For the hottest hours or longer stretches, an air-conditioned taxi, a ride-hailing car or a long-distance bus is often the more sensible choice. Keep the bike for the short, beautiful rides in the morning and evening. Heading north in December instead? Our guide to riding in Hanoi in winter deals with the opposite problem.

In short

Heat raises the risk of accidents and can become an emergency (WHO). Ride early and late, rest in the shade at midday, drink about a cup of water an hour, wear light clothing that covers your skin and keep the helmet on, as Article 33 requires.
