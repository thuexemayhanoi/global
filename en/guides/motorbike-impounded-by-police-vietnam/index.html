When Can Police Impound Your Rental Motorbike in Vietnam?

Understand when Vietnamese traffic police can impound a rental motorbike: missing licence, papers and alcohol rules, the fines involved, and how to get the bike released.

Renting a motorbike in Hanoi gives you enormous freedom, but it also puts a Vietnamese-registered vehicle in your hands that does not belong to you. If traffic police stop you and find a problem with your licence, your paperwork or your alcohol level, they can have the motorbike impounded by police in Vietnam on the spot. Knowing when that can happen, and what to do afterwards, saves you hours of stress and a difficult conversation with the rental shop.

Why impoundment matters for renters

As a renter you face two separate problems when a bike is seized. The first is practical: you are standing at the roadside without transport, and you need to understand the procedure, keep your documents safe and ask for anything you do not understand to be explained before you sign it. The second is contractual: the vehicle belongs to the rental company, and most rental agreements hold the rider responsible for whatever happens during the rental period. Before you even pick up the keys, it is worth reading our overview of rental documents and contracts in Hanoi and keeping the pickup checklist at hand.

What "tạm giữ" actually means

The Vietnamese term for police impoundment is "tạm giữ phương tiện" – the temporary seizure of a vehicle. Under clause 1 of Article 48 of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, the competent officer may hold a vehicle before the penalty decision is even issued, in order to stop a violation at once, on the basis of point b of clause 1 and clauses 2 and 8 of Article 125 of the Law on Handling Administrative Violations (Toàn văn Nghị định 168/2024/NĐ-CP). If the rider or the owner is not present, does not cooperate, does not meet the conditions to ride – for example without a valid licence – or the bike is not roadworthy, the officers move it to the place where held vehicles are kept, or hire someone to do so (clause 4 of the same article). A practical overview of the offences on the list is published by Thư viện pháp luật.

Impoundment is a preventive measure, not a punishment in itself. The logic is simple: if a rider has just committed a serious violation, letting them ride on would defeat the purpose of the stop. For you as a renter, the important consequence is time. A seized rental bike is not something you can simply walk away from, because the shop will want its vehicle back and will look at the contract to decide who bears the consequences.

The most common trigger: riding without a valid licence

The single most common reason foreign riders lose a rental bike is a licence that is not valid in Vietnam. For most visitors a home licence on its own is not enough, and an International Driving Permit only works if it covers the class of bike you are riding and is carried together with your national licence. We explain both points in detail in foreign driving licences and International Driving Permits for motorbikes.

Riding without any licence at all is treated as a serious violation. Under point a of clause 5, Article 18 of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, riding a two-wheeler with an engine of up to 125 cm³ (or an electric motor of up to eleven kilowatts) without a licence carries a fine of between 2 and 4 million VND; for machines above 125 cm³, point b of clause 7 sets the range at 6 to 8 million VND (Nghị định 168/2024/NĐ-CP). Point b of clause 5 applies the same 2 to 4 million VND range to a rider who holds an International Driving Permit from a country party to the 1968 Convention but does not carry the matching national licence. Clauses 5 and 7 of Article 18 are both on the list in Article 48 that allows the vehicle to be held (Nghị định 168/2024/NĐ-CP). A rider who cannot produce a licence is therefore exposed twice: to a substantial fine and to losing the bike for the duration of the procedure.

Note that the decree was amended recently. Decree 238/2026/NĐ-CP of 26 June 2026, in force since 15 August 2026, changed a number of provisions of Decree 168, including point a of clause 4 of Article 18 (riders aged 16 to under 18) and some wording in Article 48. It did not change the licence fines in clauses 5 and 7 of Article 18 quoted above (Thư viện pháp luật: full text of Decree 238/2026/NĐ-CP).

Alcohol and other violations that lead to impoundment

Alcohol at the handlebar is treated harshly in Vietnam, and drink-riding offences, at every alcohol level, and refusing an alcohol test are among the violations that can justify holding the vehicle under Article 48 (Nghị định 168/2024/NĐ-CP). The thresholds and the way testing works are a topic of their own, which we cover in drink-driving rules for motorbike riders. The practical rule for renters is simpler than any legal table: if you plan to ride, do not drink at all.

Beyond licences and alcohol, Article 48 lists a long catalogue of traffic violations in which holding the vehicle is permitted, drawn from the offence articles of Decree 168, including Article 18 on licences (Thư viện pháp luật). You do not need to memorise the catalogue. The pattern to remember is this: the more serious the violation and the less able you are to lawfully continue riding, the more likely the police are to take the bike into custody instead of letting you ride on.

What happens during and after the stop

When police wave you over, stay calm and cooperative. Take off your helmet, switch off the engine and hand over the documents they ask for. Article 48(3) of Decree 168 names among them the driving licence and the vehicle's registration certificate, which can be shown on paper or as information integrated in an electronic identification account (Nghị định 168/2024/NĐ-CP). If a violation is recorded, you receive a written record (biên bản) that states the deadline for coming back to resolve the case. Read it, note the name and unit of the officers, and above all call the rental shop immediately: the bike is registered in the shop's name, and experienced shops know how the local procedure works. Our guide to traffic police stops with a rental bike walks through this moment in detail.

Do not hand over money at the roadside without a written record. A penalty decision has to be complied with within ten days of receiving it, unless the decision itself gives a longer period (Thư viện pháp luật), and payment can be made online through the National Public Service Portal via a bank you select there (LuatVietnam) – see how to pay a traffic fine in Vietnam. If your violation was recorded by a camera rather than a live stop, the notice can arrive later through the rental shop, as explained in traffic-camera fines for rental motorbikes.

Getting the bike released

Clause 2 of Article 48 also allows the officer to hold the vehicle and related papers to make sure the penalty decision is carried out, or to verify facts needed for it (Toàn văn Nghị định 168/2024/NĐ-CP). In practice, the case has to be dealt with before the bike comes back. Because the bike is registered to the rental company, the shop usually has to be involved: when papers cannot be shown at the check, a separate record can also be made against the vehicle owner, and the shop may need to send or authorise a representative to collect the vehicle. Bring your passport and every document from the case when you go.

If the only problem was that you could not show your papers, clause 3 of Article 48 matters: if you produce them within the deadline written in the record, no penalty decision is issued for not having or not carrying them, and the owner is not fined either; if you come later or cannot produce them, the decision for the recorded violations has to be complied with (Nghị định 168/2024/NĐ-CP). This only helps when the papers exist – a rider without a valid licence cannot produce one later.

Local practice varies, so treat any step-by-step description you read online – including this one – as a general picture. The authoritative instructions are the ones written in the record and in the decision you receive, and following them exactly is the fastest way to get the bike back.

Who pays: renter or rental shop

The seizure itself is a police matter, but some of its costs are set by law. When a vehicle is held, the owner bears any cost of other transport for the people and goods it was carrying, and the rider or the owner pays for any towing service the officers hire to bring the vehicle to the holding place (Nghị định 168/2024/NĐ-CP, Article 48(4)). Who finally carries those costs between you and the shop depends on what your rental agreement says about traffic penalties, damage and the deposit. Before you ride, ask two questions: who pays a penalty issued to the vehicle, and who do you call if the bike is seized. A serious answer to both questions is a good sign; a shrug is a warning.

Keep every piece of paper connected to the case: the violation record, the decision, the payment receipt, and the names and times you were given. If the shop deducts anything from your deposit, documented papers are the only thing that turns an argument into a straightforward refund discussion. The starting point for these clauses is our hub on documents and contracts.

How to avoid the situation altogether

Almost every impoundment of a rental bike traces back to one of three fixable causes. Fix them before you ride and the topic becomes theoretical.

  • Sort out your licence first: an International Driving Permit with the right class, or a Vietnamese licence, carried every single time you ride.
  • Carry the vehicle papers with you. The registration certificate belongs to the shop, but it should travel with the bike: under Article 48(3) of Decree 168, a copy only counts in one narrow case, a certified copy with the original receipt of a bank that holds the original certificate (Nghị định 168/2024/NĐ-CP). See papers to carry when riding.
  • Treat alcohol and riding as mutually exclusive for the length of your trip.

None of this is complicated, which is precisely the point. A rider with a valid licence, the right papers and an empty glass is very unlikely to ever see the inside of a police storage compound – and far more likely to enjoy the ride that brought them to Hanoi in the first place.
