Damage and Theft: Who Pays in a Motorbike Rental?

Rented motorbike damaged or stolen in Hanoi? This guide explains who pays, what Vietnamese lease law says, how deposits and insurance work and what to do in the first hour after a theft.

You sign for a rental motorbike in Hanoi, ride away, and two questions sit quietly inside every contract you will ever sign here: who pays if the bike comes back scratched, and who pays if it does not come back at all? Damage and theft liability is the part of a rental agreement most travellers read last and regret first. This guide walks through the rules that actually apply in Vietnam: what the law says about looking after a leased vehicle, what a fair contract should regulate, how deposits are handled, and what to do in the first hour after a theft. It belongs to our overview of rental documents and contracts.

What Vietnamese law says about damage to a leased vehicle

Vietnam's Civil Code of 2015 defines a property lease in Article 472 as an agreement in which one party makes a good available to another for use over a fixed term in exchange for payment. Article 478 sets out the lessee's duties: keep the leased property safe and maintained, use it according to its intended purpose, and compensate for damage or loss caused by the lessee's own fault. The wording and a practical commentary on these articles are in the official explanation of lease contracts under the Civil Code 2015. For a rented motorbike, two things follow. First, whoever causes damage is, as a baseline, the one who answers for it – no contract can shift that responsibility entirely onto the owner. Second, fair wear is not damage: brake pads, tyres and oil wear out through proper use, and that cost belongs to the owner, not to your travel budget. Every dispute at return time starts exactly on the line between normal use and negligence.

What a fair rental contract should regulate

A serious rental shop in Hanoi puts the liability questions in writing before you take the bike. What you want to read before signing is who bears each category of harm: crash damage you cause yourself, damage done by third parties while the bike is parked, theft during the rental period, and defects that were already present at handover. A fair contract also says how a damage amount is assessed: a workshop invoice is verifiable, an arbitrary lump sum is not. If your plans change later, the rules for amending or ending the agreement are covered in our guide to rental agreements, changes and cancellations.

Camera notices are a separate topic. The motorbike is normally registered to the rental shop, not to you, so the contract should say who ultimately pays for a notice and how the cost is passed on to you, as we explain in our guide to camera notices on a rental motorbike. And if you plan to let a travel companion ride the bike, agree on it in advance; what happens when someone else rides your motorbike is described in letting someone else ride your motorbike in Vietnam.

Scratches, crash damage and the deposit

Documentation decides these cases. Photograph the bike all around at handover, including the scratches that were already there, and note the odometer. If new damage happens during the rental, report it to the shop immediately instead of explaining it at return: a documented, promptly reported scratch costs less in every currency than a discovered one. If the owner deducts damage from your deposit, ask for the evidence: what was repaired, what it cost, and which part of it was wear rather than damage. How a correct refund works and what to check before you accept a deduction is covered in motorbike rental deposits and refund terms; damage as a cost beyond the rent itself sits in beyond the rental fee, inside our overview of rental costs and fees.

If the bike is stolen: the first hour

Work in a fixed order. First, secure your own belongings and pin down the last known location of the bike. Second, report the theft at the police station of the ward where the bike was last parked and ask for a written record – that document is what the owner and, if relevant, an insurer will ask for. Third, inform the rental shop immediately and keep a copy of the report together with time and place. Not every missing motorbike has been stolen: if police impounded it because of a missing licence, missing papers or an alcohol check, you are in a different procedure, described in when police can impound your motorbike in Vietnam.

If your passport is sitting in the shop's drawer as a deposit, a theft dispute becomes extra stressful: an unresolved theft question can delay the return of your document, and without a passport every visit to an authority turns into a patience test. The risks of that practice and the alternatives are set out in leaving your passport as a rental deposit.

What insurance covers – and what it does not

Every registered motor vehicle in Vietnam must carry compulsory civil-liability insurance; the insured conditions, the premiums and the minimum insured sums are regulated by Decree 67/2023/NĐ-CP (full text of the decree, thuvienphapluat.vn). That policy covers liability towards third parties – people and property the vehicle harms. It does not pay for the theft of the bike itself, and it does not pay for repairs to the bike itself. In a theft, the owner loses the vehicle, and what you owe as the renter is decided by the contract alone. How that compulsory policy works on a rental bike is explained in detail in compulsory motorbike insurance in Vietnam. Travel insurance, by contrast, can cover personal belongings stolen along with the bike and medical costs after a crash – what to verify is in does travel insurance cover riding a motorbike in Vietnam?, including the exclusion that bites most riders: cover often depends on holding a licence valid for the bike.

Frequently asked questions

Who pays if my rented motorbike is stolen in Vietnam? Your rental contract decides it: in a theft the owner loses the vehicle, and what you owe as the renter is set by the contract alone. Read the theft clause before you sign, including what happens to your deposit. If the bike is stolen, report it at the police station of the ward where it was last parked, ask for a written record and inform the shop immediately.

Do I have to pay for worn tyres or brake pads on a rental motorbike? No, fair wear is not damage. Brake pads, tyres and oil wear out through proper use, and that cost belongs to the owner. Under Article 478 of the Civil Code, a renter compensates for damage or loss caused by their own fault, as the official explanation of lease contracts under the Civil Code 2015 sets out.

What should I do if I scratch the rental bike during the rental? Report it to the shop straight away instead of explaining it at return, because a documented, promptly reported scratch costs less than one the shop discovers later. Photograph the damage when it happens. If the owner then deducts the repair from your deposit, ask for the evidence: what was repaired, what it cost and which part of it was wear rather than damage.

Before you sign: a five-point routine

  • Read the liability clauses in full: who bears crash damage, parking damage, theft and pre-existing defects?
  • Photograph the bike all around at handover, including the damage that was already there.
  • Agree in writing who is allowed to ride the bike during the rental.
  • Ask for the theft clause: what you owe if the bike is stolen, and what happens to your deposit.
  • Read the deposit terms through to the refund promise, not just to the amount.
