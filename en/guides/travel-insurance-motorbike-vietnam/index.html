Does Travel Insurance Cover Riding a Motorbike in Vietnam?

UK Foreign Office advice, why your licence and permit decide your cover, what Vietnam's compulsory insurance leaves out and what to ask your insurer.

Most travellers buy insurance and assume it covers whatever happens on holiday. Riding a motorbike in Vietnam is where that assumption is most often tested. Whether a claim after a crash is paid depends on the wording of your policy, and also on something you control before you start the engine: whether you are riding legally at all. This guide sets out what the UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO) advises, what Vietnamese law requires, what Vietnam's compulsory motorbike insurance does and does not do, and the questions to put to your insurer before you rent. For the paperwork side of renting, see our hub on rental documents and contracts, and run through the pickup checklist when you collect the bike.

What the UK Foreign Office says

The FCDO's Vietnam travel advice tells travellers to get appropriate travel insurance that covers their itinerary, planned activities and expenses in an emergency. Its safety and security page is blunter about motorbikes: travelling by motorbike in Vietnam is much riskier than in the UK, accidents are common and have killed and injured British nationals, and you should not hire a motorbike if you are not an experienced rider. In the same section it adds a one-line reminder that sums up this whole guide: make sure your travel insurance covers your planned activity.

The health page explains why that matters. Your policy should cover local treatment and unexpected medical evacuation, including an air ambulance if you need to be flown home, and full medical cover, because medical bills can be expensive and the British Embassy does not pay them. If you are referred to a medical facility, the FCDO advises contacting your insurance company quickly.

The licence matters before the policy wording does

Before you compare policies, check that you can ride legally, because a policy cannot turn an unlicensed ride into a covered one. Vietnam's Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety recognises an international driving permit only when it was issued by a country that is party to the 1968 Vienna Convention on Road Traffic, and only together with your national licence for the same class of vehicle (Article 57 of the consolidated law). The FCDO puts it in practical terms: you need both the 1968 version of the international driving permit and your UK driving licence with you while driving a car or motorbike in Vietnam (FCDO safety and security).

The class matters as much as the paper. Under the same article, two-wheel motorbikes up to 125 cm³, or with an electric motor up to 11 kW, need category A1, and anything more powerful needs category A. A permit that only shows car entitlement does not cover the 110 to 125 cm³ scooters most rental shops offer. The exception is a moped (xe gắn máy) with a design speed of no more than 50 km/h and an engine of up to 50 cm³ or 4 kW, which needs no licence for riders aged 16 or over under Articles 34, 56 and 59. Our guides to foreign licences and international driving permits explain the classes in more detail.

Vietnam's compulsory insurance protects other people, not you

Every motor vehicle in Vietnam must have compulsory civil liability insurance, and a rider must carry the certificate together with the vehicle registration and a suitable licence (Article 56 of the road traffic law, as explained on the Government portal). Under Decree 67/2023/NĐ-CP, this insurance covers the vehicle owner's liability for injury, death and property damage caused to third parties, and for injury or death of passengers on the vehicle. The rider is not a third party under the decree, so your own injuries and the rental bike itself fall outside it.

The decree also caps what it pays: VND 150 million per person per accident for injury or death, and VND 50 million per accident for property damage caused by a motorbike (Decree 67/2023/NĐ-CP, Article 6). More important for renters, Article 7 says the insurer is not liable when the rider has no licence, an invalid licence or one that does not match the vehicle, and it excludes property damage when the rider had alcohol above the normal level. In those situations, an injured person's claim can land on you personally.

An accident can cost more than medical bills

The FCDO warns that if you are involved in an accident you could face criminal charges and may need to pay compensation to injured people even if the injuries are minor, and that foreign nationals have been stopped from leaving Vietnam until police investigations were complete, sometimes being asked to agree out-of-court financial settlements (FCDO safety and security). It also notes that drink-driving is a serious offence: if you are tested and found to have any alcohol in your system, you may get a fine and possible imprisonment. Personal liability cover in a travel policy can matter in these situations, but only if its terms apply to riding a motor vehicle.

Questions to ask your insurer before you ride

Read the full policy wording rather than the summary, and get written answers to these questions:

  • Is riding a motorbike or scooter covered when you are the rider, or only as a passenger?
  • Is there an engine-size limit, and does it include the 110 to 125 cm³ bikes you are likely to rent?
  • Does cover require a licence that is valid in Vietnam for that class of bike, plus the international driving permit?
  • Is wearing a helmet a condition of cover? The FCDO notes that it is illegal for drivers and passengers to ride without one.
  • How are accidents treated when the rider has drunk any alcohol?
  • Does personal liability cover apply to accidents involving a motor vehicle you were riding?
  • What are the limits for medical evacuation and repatriation, and is an air ambulance included?
  • Is there a 24-hour assistance line, and what should you do before agreeing to a hospital transfer or payment?

If something happens

In an emergency, call 115 for an ambulance and 113 for the police (what Vietnam's emergency numbers are). Contact your insurer's assistance line as soon as you can, ideally before agreeing to transfers or large payments, keep every medical record and receipt, and photograph the scene, the bikes and any documents. Do not hand over your passport as a guarantee; the FCDO notes that rental shops or landlords can hold a passport against claimed damage. If the problem is mechanical rather than medical, our guide to rental bike breakdowns explains the next steps.

The short answer

Travel insurance can cover a motorbike accident in Vietnam only if your policy includes riding, you hold the right licence and permit for the bike, you wear a helmet and you ride sober. If any of those is missing, assume you are not covered and book a ride-hailing bike or a driver instead.
