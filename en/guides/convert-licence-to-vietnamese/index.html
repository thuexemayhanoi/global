Converting a Foreign Licence to a Vietnamese Motorbike Licence

How residents convert a foreign licence into a Vietnamese one under Circular 12/2025: who qualifies, which motorbike class you get and where to apply.

If you are living in Vietnam rather than passing through, an international driving permit is not the long-term answer. Vietnamese rules expect residents who hold a licence from another country to convert it into a Vietnamese one, and the motorbike class you end up with depends entirely on what your home licence already covers. This guide walks through who can convert, what to prepare, where to apply and what usually goes wrong. It is written for people who will be renting or buying a motorbike over months, not days; if you are collecting a rental this week, the Hanoi motorbike rental checklist is the more practical starting point.

Who can convert a foreign licence

Since 1 July 2026 the rules are in Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA of the Ministry of Public Security, which replaced Circular 12/2025/TT-BCA of 28 February 2025. The earlier circular already set the principle, as a summary explains: foreigners who hold a national licence and want to drive in Vietnam convert it into the corresponding Vietnamese class, unless an international treaty Vietnam has joined says otherwise.

Article 24 of Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA sets the conditions. A foreigner who lives, works or studies in Vietnam can apply with:

  • a national driving licence that is still valid; and
  • a diplomatic or official identity card, a visa or temporary residence card still valid for at least 90 days, or a valid permanent residence card (Article 24(2)(c)).

The international permit point is worth underlining. You convert the national card issued by your home country, not the IDP booklet. The earlier circular's Article 24(1) also excluded temporary licences, expired licences, licences that are damaged, altered or too worn to read, licences with identity discrepancies and licences not issued by a competent authority, so make sure your card is current and legible before you start.

The residence requirement is what rules out short-stay visitors. Australia's Smartraveller puts it in plain terms for its citizens: converting an Australian licence requires a valid Vietnamese residence permit of at least three months. Spain's foreign ministry likewise notes that Spanish residents in Vietnam can exchange their licence and drive legally, while Spanish tourists cannot use their international permit.

Which motorbike class you will receive

Conversion gives you the Vietnamese class that corresponds to what your licence already allows. It does not add new categories. Under Article 57 of Law 36/2024/QH15 (consolidated text), class A1 covers two-wheel motorcycles up to 125 cm³ or with an electric motor up to 11 kW, and class A covers larger motorcycles as well as everything in A1. Car classes start at B.

The practical consequence is spelled out in the Embassy of Japan's guidance for its nationals. If you convert a licence that only covers ordinary cars, the Vietnamese licence you receive also covers only cars, and you cannot ride a motorbike with it. To ride, you either need a home licence that includes motorcycles, or you must convert your car licence and then pass the Vietnamese motorbike theory and riding tests separately. The same embassy page notes that a Japanese automatic-only car licence cannot be converted to a Vietnamese car licence at all.

Before you start, look at the categories printed on your home licence. If you see a motorcycle category such as A or A1 in a European-style licence, or the equivalent motorcycle class in your country's system, you have something to convert. If you only see car categories, conversion alone will not let you ride a scooter in Hanoi.

Documents to prepare

Article 24(2) of Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA lists the application file, much as the Dong Nai police traffic department described it for applicants under the earlier circular:

  • the application form for foreigners;
  • a Vietnamese translation of your foreign licence, certified by a notary office in Vietnam or by the Vietnamese diplomatic mission where the translator works, sealed across the attached copy of the licence;
  • a driver's health certificate from a qualified medical facility, still valid (the circular exempts foreigners converting for the period stated on their entry visa or temporary residence card);
  • a copy of your diplomatic or official identity card, a visa or temporary residence card still valid for at least 90 days, or a valid permanent residence card.

When you submit the file you must show the originals of these documents, and your photo is taken at the counter rather than supplied by you. Bring your passport as well. The Embassy of Japan's list for its nationals includes the passport, and it is the document officials use to match your identity across the other papers.

The translation is where most delays start. It must be a certified translation of the licence itself, not an informal one, and the seal must join the translation to the copy of the licence. Ask the notary office to do both at once.

Where to apply, how long it takes and the fee

Under Article 24(3) of Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA, you file in person at the provincial police traffic department (Phòng Cảnh sát giao thông) or at a commune-level police office that has been set up as a reception point, or online through the National Public Service Portal. Officials check the file, enter it electronically and give you an appointment slip; if they refuse, they must explain why in writing.

The circular then sets the timing. Once a complete, valid file is received, the data go to the national Traffic Police Department, which issues the electronic licence and adds it to the national electronic identification app within one day; if you want a printed card, it is printed and handed over within two and a half working days (Article 24(3)(d)). You can collect it where you applied or ask for it to be posted. The Embassy of Japan's page, updated in July 2026, describes the process for Hanoi applicants as taking up to seven working days after the file is accepted, so plan for some margin.

For Hanoi, the Embassy of Japan names the Ministry of Public Security's Traffic Police Department licensing team at No. 2 Phùng Hưng, Hà Đông, open on weekday mornings and afternoons and on Saturday mornings, with a queue ticket system. Reception arrangements change, so check with the office before you go.

The fee for converting a licence is 135,000 VND per issuance, according to the Dong Nai police traffic department; the Embassy of Japan page gives the same figure as of July 2026. Translation, notarisation and the medical check are paid separately to the providers you use.

How long the converted licence lasts

A Vietnamese motorbike licence issued directly in class A1 or A has no expiry date for Vietnamese holders, but a converted licence for a foreigner is tied to your circumstances. The Embassy of Japan states that the licence it describes is valid until your Vietnamese residence permit expires or your Japanese licence expires, whichever comes first. Check the expiry printed on your own card when you collect it, and put a reminder in your calendar to deal with renewal at the same time as your residence card.

Remember that the home licence still matters after conversion. If it expires and you later need to convert again, you will be asked for a valid foreign licence. Keep it renewed even if you do not plan to drive at home.

When conversion is not possible

Some people cannot convert, either because they hold neither a visa or residence card valid for at least 90 more days nor a permanent residence card, or because their licence covers only cars. In that case the options are limited:

  • Take the Vietnamese tests. The U.S. Department of State tells its citizens that to get a Vietnamese driver's licence they should contact the Traffic Police Office of the Provincial Public Security and that a medical exam may be needed.
  • Use an international permit if you are only visiting and your country issues the 1968 Vienna Convention model, or if a bilateral agreement covers you. That route has its own rules and is not a substitute for conversion once you live here.
  • Do not ride. Ride-hailing, taxis and tours with a driver cover most of what a resident needs while the paperwork is pending.

Riding without the right licence is not a technicality. The U.S. State Department warns that driving without a Vietnamese licence can lead to fines, arrest or jail, and Smartraveller says travel insurers are likely to reject claims from unlicensed riders.

Frequently asked questions

Can I convert my international driving permit into a Vietnamese licence? No. What you convert is the national card issued by your home country, which must still be valid, not the international permit; the earlier Circular 12/2025/TT-BCA listed international driving permits among the documents that cannot be converted (Article 24(1)). Under Article 24(2)(c) of Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA, in force since 1 July 2026, you also need a diplomatic or official identity card, a visa or temporary residence card still valid for at least 90 days, or a valid permanent residence card.

Will converting a car-only licence let me ride a motorbike in Vietnam? No. Conversion gives you the Vietnamese class that matches what your home licence already allows and does not add new categories. The Embassy of Japan's guidance explains that a converted car-only licence covers only cars, so to ride you need a home licence that includes motorcycles, or you must convert the car licence and then pass the Vietnamese motorbike theory and riding tests separately.

Do I need a medical certificate to convert a foreign licence in Vietnam? Usually yes. The application file listed by the Dong Nai police traffic department includes a valid driver's health certificate from a qualified medical facility. The circular exempts foreigners converting for the period stated on their entry visa or temporary residence card, so check whether that exemption applies to you before you book the health check.

A realistic timeline

1. Confirm that your visa or residence card is valid for at least 90 more days (or that you hold a permanent residence card) and that your home licence covers motorcycles. 2. Get a certified Vietnamese translation of the licence, sealed to a copy. 3. Book the health check if the exemption does not apply to you. 4. Prepare a copy of your visa or residence card. 5. Submit the file in person, have your photo taken and pay the fee. 6. Collect the card or wait for it by post, then check the class and expiry date.

Once the licence is in your hand, the same checks apply to every rental: ask for the registration and insurance papers and record the bike's condition. For general information about renting in Hanoi, return to the English guide home page.
