Foreign Driving Licences and Motorbike Classes in Vietnam

Check when a foreign motorbike licence or international driving permit applies in Vietnam, and how vehicle class, validity and conversion affect riding.

"Will my licence work in Vietnam?" is the first question most visitors ask before renting a motorbike in Hanoi, and the honest answer depends on three things: which convention your country's international driving permit follows, which category is printed on your licence, and the engine size of the bike you want. This guide sets out the rule, shows how official sources describe the position of several countries, explains the Vietnamese classes and the 125 cc line, and lists the fines and consequences of getting it wrong. What a Hanoi shop will ask to see and what belongs in the contract is covered in our Hanoi motorbike rental pickup checklist.

The rule: a 1968 permit plus your national licence

Vietnam is a party to the 1968 Vienna Convention on Road Traffic. In practice that means a foreign licence is recognised when it is presented together with an international driving permit issued under the 1968 Convention, and only for the categories shown on the national licence. The UK Foreign Office states it plainly: you need to have both the 1968 version of the international driving permit and your UK driving licence with you while driving a car or motorbike in Vietnam, and it is illegal to drive without third-party insurance. The German Foreign Office describes the same arrangement for temporary stays and adds that riding motorbikes or scooters above 50 cc on a car-only class B licence is not permitted. The permit is not a licence in itself; it is a translation of the categories you already hold, so a car-only licence gives you no right to a 110 cc scooter whatever the permit says on its cover.

Which countries' permits work and which do not

Official sources draw clear lines. Countries that issue 1968-Convention permits, including the UK, Germany, France, the Netherlands, Spain and Portugal, are covered by the rule above. Two large groups are not. The U.S. Department of State says that U.S. driver's licenses and international driving permits do not work in Vietnam and that driving without a Vietnamese licence can lead to fines, arrest or jail. The Japanese Embassy in Vietnam tells its citizens in its safety guide that Japanese driving licences and international driving permits are not valid in Vietnam and that a Vietnamese licence is required. South Korea is a special case: the Korean Ministry of Foreign Affairs lists Vietnam among the countries with which Korea has a mutual-recognition agreement for international driving permits, so Korean permits are usable despite Korea following the 1949 Geneva Convention. If your country is not covered by one of these sources, check its own foreign-ministry advice rather than a rental shop's assurance; many shops will hand over a bike to anyone, and the risk stays with you.

Vietnamese licence classes and the 125 cc line

Vietnam's classes were redefined by Law 36/2024/QH15, in force since 1 January 2025. According to the Nghệ An administrative service centre, class A1 covers two-wheelers up to 125 cc or electric motorcycles up to 11 kW, class A covers everything above and includes A1 vehicles, and class B1 covers three-wheeled motorcycles. The 125 cc line matters enormously for renters: a Honda Vision, Air Blade 125, Lead or Wave sits inside A1, while a Winner or an Exciter 155 needs class A. Map your own licence onto that line. A European A1 category matches Vietnam's A1; A2 or A matches Vietnam's A; AM or any moped-only category covers nothing above 50 cc. Riders with a full motorcycle licence from a 1968 country can rent anything; riders whose permit shows only A1 should stay below 125 cc.

Mopeds under 50 cc: the one licence-free category

Below the classes sits a category that needs no licence at all. The Ministry of Public Security explains that a two-wheeler with a design speed of no more than 50 km/h and an engine of no more than 50 cc, or an electric motor of no more than 4 kW, is a "xe gắn máy" that anyone aged 16 or over may ride without a driving licence, provided they carry the registration certificate and the compulsory insurance certificate. This is the legal basis for the "no licence needed" offers you will see in Hanoi. Read it carefully: all three limits must be met, so an electric scooter with a top speed above 50 km/h is a motorcycle requiring class A1 even if its motor is small. A genuine 50 cc petrol scooter or a low-speed electric model is the only legal self-drive option for visitors whose licence is not recognised.

Converting to a Vietnamese licence for longer stays

The permit route is designed for visitors. Anyone living in Vietnam on a residence card should look at converting their licence into a Vietnamese one. The State Department advises contacting the Traffic Police Office of the Provincial Public Security to obtain a Vietnamese licence, notes that a medical examination may be required, and points to the Ministry of Public Security's public-service portal, which is in Vietnamese only. Requirements, documents and the responsible office change from time to time, so confirm them locally or through your embassy before relying on them; a certified translation of your licence and proof of residence are routinely requested.

Fines and consequences of riding without the right class

Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP raised the penalties sharply from 2025. According to the Thư Viện Nhà Đất summary, riding without a licence matching the vehicle costs 2,000,000 to 4,000,000 VND on a bike up to 125 cc and 6,000,000 to 8,000,000 VND above that, and simply not carrying the licence, the registration or the insurance certificate costs 200,000 to 300,000 VND per offence. The money is the smaller problem. The UK Foreign Office warns that after an accident you could face criminal charges, be required to pay compensation to injured people even for minor injuries, and be subject to a travel ban during the investigation; the German Foreign Office mentions multi-year prison terms after serious accidents without a valid licence. Travel insurers routinely require a valid licence for the vehicle class as a condition of cover, so an unlicensed rider may also be uninsured for medical costs and evacuation.

A practical checklist before you rent

  • Does your country issue a 1968-Convention permit, or does an official source say your permit is accepted in Vietnam? If not, do not rent anything above 50 cc.
  • Does your permit show a motorcycle category, and does it cover the engine size you want: A1 up to 125 cc, A2 or A above?
  • Are you carrying both the permit and the national licence on every ride, together with the bike's registration and insurance certificate?
  • Staying on a residence card? Plan the conversion to a Vietnamese licence instead of relying on the permit.
  • Does your travel insurance cover motorised two-wheelers with the licence class you hold?

Settle those five points before you leave home and you ride in Hanoi legally and insured; skip them and you are relying on a rental shop's goodwill, which is worth nothing at the next checkpoint or the first accident.
