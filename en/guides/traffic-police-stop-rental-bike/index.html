Stopped by Traffic Police on a Rental Bike: What to Expect

When Vietnamese traffic police may stop you, what they check, your rights under the 2024 law, how to handle the stop and how camera fines reach you.

Sooner or later, many visitors who ride in Vietnam see a traffic police officer step into the road and signal them to pull over. A traffic police stop is usually short and routine, but it goes much better when you know why you were stopped, what the officer will ask for and what you are entitled to. This guide is based on Vietnam's Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety, which took effect on 1 January 2025, and on the UK Foreign Office's advice for travellers. For the paperwork you should carry from the moment you rent, see our hub on rental documents and contracts, and use the pickup checklist when you collect the bike.

When the police may stop you

The law lists four grounds on which traffic police may stop a vehicle to check it (Article 66 of the consolidated law):

  • they see a violation, or have grounds to believe one has been committed;
  • they are carrying out an authorised patrol and control plan that requires stopping vehicles to detect violations;
  • they are protecting national security, public order or fighting crime, or responding to disasters, fires, rescues or epidemics;
  • they have received a report, denunciation or request about a crime or another violation.

In practice, the second ground means a stop at a checkpoint does not imply that you did anything wrong. Patrols also rely on cameras, surveillance systems, technical devices and data, so a violation can be recorded without anyone stopping you at all (Article 67).

What they will check

Expect to be asked for the documents every rider must carry: the vehicle registration certificate, a driving licence for the class of vehicle you are riding, and the compulsory civil liability insurance certificate, plus a technical inspection certificate for vehicles that the law requires to be inspected (Article 56). For a foreign rider, the licence question is the one that matters most. Vietnam recognises an international driving permit only together with your national licence for the same class, and two-wheel motorbikes up to 125 cm³ need category A1 (Article 57). The UK Foreign Office's safety and security advice is explicit: you need both the 1968 version of the international driving permit and your UK licence with you when you drive a car or ride a motorbike.

Officers also look at the obvious: a fastened helmet for rider and passenger, and alcohol. The FCDO warns that drink-driving is a serious offence and that if you are tested and found to have any alcohol in your system, you may get a fine and possible imprisonment.

Your rights and your duties

Article 72 sets out both sides. You must obey the signal to stop and the officers' requests to check you and your vehicle. In return, you have the right to be told the grounds for stopping you, the content and result of the check, the violation found and the measures being taken; to explain yourself; and to complain about or challenge an administrative decision under the law. You may also report misconduct during patrols through the official channels.

How to handle the stop calmly

1. Pull over where the officer indicates, switch off the engine and keep your hands visible. 2. Hand over the documents you were asked for, and nothing else. If your licence is integrated into a Vietnamese electronic ID account, it can be shown that way (Article 56), which mainly applies to residents. 3. Ask which violation is alleged. You are entitled to know, and it helps if you later need to explain or complain. 4. Read any record before signing it. If you do not understand the Vietnamese text, say so and ask for an explanation. 5. Keep your passport. The FCDO advises not to use your passport as a guarantee that you will pay a fine for a traffic offence (FCDO).

Fines are set by Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP. Because amounts vary by violation and vehicle, the useful rule is simple: pay only against an official decision that names the violation.

Camera fines can follow you after you return the bike

Not every violation is handled on the spot. When cameras or technical devices record a violation and the vehicle cannot be stopped, the authorities send a notice to the vehicle owner, in writing or through the Ministry of Public Security's traffic app, and publish the vehicle on the Traffic Police Department's website. The owner must cooperate to identify who was riding, and an owner who cannot show that someone else was riding is fined. Unresolved cases are reported to the registration and licensing authorities, which then hold back their services until the matter is settled (Ministry of Public Security answer on Decree 168, Article 47).

For a renter, that means the shop can identify you from the rental contract and your documents, even after you have left. Ask at the return whether any notice has arrived, keep a photo of the contract and the shop's contact details, and settle any genuine notice through the official process rather than in cash at the counter.

If you do not have the right licence

If you hold only a car licence, or an international driving permit that does not cover motorbikes, a stop can quickly become a fine for riding without a valid licence under Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, and an accident can become far more serious. Our guides to foreign licences and international driving permits explain what you can legally ride. If the answer is nothing bigger than a 50 cm³ moped, a ride-hailing bike is the safer choice for getting around.

Quick checklist before you ride

  • Original registration certificate and a valid compulsory insurance certificate for this bike.
  • A licence for the right class, plus the 1968 international driving permit if you are a visitor.
  • Helmets fastened for rider and passenger.
  • No alcohol at all before riding.
  • A photo of the rental contract and the shop's phone number.
