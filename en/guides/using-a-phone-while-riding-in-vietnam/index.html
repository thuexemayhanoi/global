Using a Phone While Riding a Motorbike in Vietnam

What Vietnam's traffic law says about phones, navigation and earphones on a motorbike, the fine and licence points under Decree 168/2024, and how to navigate a rental bike within the rules.

Almost nobody finds their way around Hanoi without a map app, and anyone who rents a motorbike wants to navigate on the move. That raises one of the most common questions from visitors: may the phone sit on the handlebars, may I listen to directions through earphones, and what happens if I tap the map for a second? This guide separates what Vietnamese traffic law expressly bans from what its wording still allows, gives the fines under Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP and shows a way to navigate that stays within the rules. For an overview of safe riding, see our topic page on safer riding and breakdown support.

What the law bans

Two provisions of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety matter for riders with a smartphone. The first is in the list of prohibited acts: under Article 9(6) it is forbidden to hold and use a phone or other electronic device in your hand while operating a vehicle that is moving on the road. The consolidated text the government published in 2026 already includes the amendments made by Law 118/2025/QH15, which apply from 1 July 2026.

The second provision applies to motorbikes and mopeds specifically. Under Article 33(3)(c), riders may not use an umbrella or audio devices; the only exception is hearing aids. Earphones, earbuds playing music or a call, and headsets are therefore covered by the ban, whether the phone is in your hand or in your pocket.

Fines and licence points

Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP puts both bans into effect in Article 7. Under Article 7(4)(đ), a rider who uses an umbrella or an audio device other than a hearing aid, or who holds and uses a phone or other electronic device while riding, faces a fine of 800,000 to 1,000,000 VND. Article 7(13)(b) adds a deduction of 4 points from the driving licence for the same offence (Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP).

The fine is often not the biggest problem. A rider who is stopped also has to show the other papers, and riding without a valid licence weighs far more heavily than the phone. Our guide on being stopped by traffic police on a rental bike explains what happens at a roadside check.

What the wording still allows

The wording of Article 9(6) is deliberately narrow: what is banned is holding and using the device by hand while the vehicle moves. Three situations therefore fall outside it:

  • A phone in a fixed mount that you do not touch while riding. If you tap, swipe or take the phone out of the mount on the move, you are using it by hand again.
  • A look at the map while stationary, for example after you have pulled over safely. Stop only where stopping is allowed, not in the middle of a junction or on the pavement.
  • A passenger who navigates. The bans are aimed at the person riding the bike. Passengers have their own rules, such as the ban on holding an umbrella (Article 33(4)).

This is no free pass. A phone in a mount still distracts, and Article 33(3) prohibits any other behaviour that endangers traffic order and safety. The safest option is still to check the route before you set off.

Grey areas: red lights, helmet headsets and photos

At a red light. Article 9(6) refers to a vehicle that is moving. The law does not say expressly whether a quick look at the phone while waiting at a light is covered. Because you have to move off at any moment, and because in practice the officer on the spot draws the line, it is wiser not to pick the phone up there either.

Helmet headsets and Bluetooth speakers. Intercoms built into helmets are audio devices too. Article 33(3)(c) exempts only hearing aids, so avoid calls and music through the helmet while riding, just as you would avoid earbuds.

Photos and videos. Taking photos or filming with the phone while riding means holding and using it, which is exactly the banned case. Leave pictures to your passenger or stop first.

A legal navigation setup for the rental bike

A few simple steps give you navigation that fits the law:

1. Check the mount. Ask the rental shop for a sturdy handlebar mount or bring your own. The phone must not fall out over potholes, otherwise you will grab for it by reflex. 2. Set the route before you ride. Enter the destination before moving off and choose a map view you can read without zooming. 3. No earphones. Spoken directions through earbuds are not allowed under Article 33. Use the screen, or memorise the next few turns before you leave. 4. Stop instead of tapping. If you take a wrong turn, pull over somewhere suitable and re-plan. In Hanoi that is almost always quicker than a risky manoeuvre. 5. Keep power and data. A power bank and a local SIM or eSIM stop the map from dying halfway through the trip.

Why the rule makes sense in Hanoi

Hanoi's traffic is dense, scooters change position constantly and pedestrians cross between the vehicles. A glance down at the screen takes only a moment, but in that moment a motorbike covers several metres. Take one hand off the handlebars as well and you have less control in a sudden stop. The bans target exactly this combination of distraction and a missing hand on the bars. Our guide to Hanoi traffic for first-time riders explains how the flow works and why anticipation matters so much.

A rental bike also feels unfamiliar for the first few days: brake pressure, throttle response and mirrors differ from what you know at home. Splitting your attention with a screen in that phase spreads it too thin. Keep the first rides short and on routes you know, memorise the route beforehand, and build up once the bike and the traffic feel familiar.

In short

Holding and using a phone while riding is banned (Article 9(6)), earphones are not allowed at all on a motorbike (Article 33), and Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP sets a fine of 800,000 to 1,000,000 VND plus a 4-point deduction. A fixed mount, a route planned in advance and stopping instead of tapping keep you on the safe side.
