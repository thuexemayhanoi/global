How to Ride a Manual Motorbike with a Clutch

A step-by-step practice plan for riders used to automatic scooters: controls, the friction zone, first-gear starts, shifting up and down, hill starts and stalls before renting a manual bike.

Many visitors who plan a motorbike loop through northern Vietnam have only ever ridden a twist-and-go scooter. A manual motorbike adds two controls that scooters do not have: a clutch lever and a foot-operated gear lever. Neither is difficult, but both need practice, and a steep mountain road or a crowded junction in Hanoi is the wrong place to learn. This guide sets out a practice plan you can work through before your trip, based on official rider training material from the UK and the US. If you are still deciding between bike types, start with our comparison of manual and automatic rentals and the topic page on choosing a rental motorbike.

First: licence and honest self-assessment

A manual bike does not change which licence you need, but its engine size does. Under Article 57(1) of Vietnam's Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety, class A1 covers motorbikes up to 125 cm³ and class A anything larger, so a 150 cm³ manual bike needs class A. Our guides to foreign driving licences and International Driving Permits explain what that means for visitors.

Be honest about your experience, too. The UK government's travel advice for Vietnam points out that riding a motorbike is riskier and advises against hiring one if you are inexperienced (FCDO). The best way to learn clutch control is a proper course at home. In the UK, compulsory basic training covers most of the skills in this plan: the controls (GOV.UK, CBT element B) and moving away, slow riding, a figure of eight, U-turns, emergency stops and gear changes (GOV.UK, CBT element C).

The controls you will use

The UK training syllabus lists the main controls a new rider must know: the throttle, front brake, clutch, indicators, choke, electric starter, engine cut-off switch, lights, horn, rear brake, gear change lever and instrument panel. It also stresses the difference between a twist-and-go scooter and a motorcycle with a gearbox (GOV.UK, CBT element B).

On most manual motorbikes the clutch is the lever on the left handlebar and the gear lever sits in front of your left foot, while the front brake lever and the throttle are on the right and the rear brake pedal is by your right foot. Layouts vary, so ask the rental shop to show you the controls on your bike before you ride off.

Step 1: Get on, find neutral and start the engine

The Oregon motorcycle manual describes a routine worth copying (Oregon DMV):

1. Mount from the left side, hold both grips, squeeze the front brake so the bike cannot roll and swing your right leg over. Sit up, raise the side stand and adjust the mirrors. 2. Turn the ignition on and shift to neutral. Do not rely on the indicator light: rock the bike back and forth, and if it rolls freely it is in neutral. 3. Turn the engine cut-off switch on and, on a cold engine, use the choke if the bike has one. 4. Squeeze the clutch before pressing the starter. Many bikes require it, and it protects you from starting in gear. 5. Press the starter without opening the throttle. Many bikes cut the engine if you select a gear with the side stand down.

Step 2: Find the friction zone

The friction zone is the point in the clutch lever's travel where the engine's power begins to reach the rear wheel (Oregon DMV). Every manual start uses it. As you let the lever out and reach the friction zone, roll on a little throttle and the bike starts to move; do not let the lever out completely until the bike is moving and stable.

A good first exercise on a flat, empty car park: with the engine running, squeeze the clutch, select first gear and let the lever out very slowly until you feel the bike want to creep forward. Pull the lever back in, and repeat until you can find that point without looking.

Step 3: Move off and stop in a straight line

Now let the bike move a few metres in first gear, then stop. Use both brakes every time: according to the Oregon manual, the front brake can provide 70 percent or more of the total stopping power, and grabbing the front lever or stamping on the rear pedal can lock a wheel (Oregon DMV). Squeeze the clutch before the bike comes to a halt so the engine does not stall. The UK syllabus expects learners to ride in a straight line, come to a planned stop and know what to do if the engine stalls (GOV.UK, CBT element C).

Step 4: Shift up

Once starts feel natural, ride on and change into second gear. The Oregon manual breaks an upshift into four movements (Oregon DMV):

  • close the throttle as you squeeze the clutch;
  • lift the gear lever with firm pressure;
  • release the gear lever;
  • ease the clutch out as you roll the throttle back on.

Step 5: Shift down and come to a stop

Downshifting is the mirror image: close the throttle as you squeeze the clutch, press the gear lever down firmly without stamping, and ease the clutch out so the rear tyre does not skid. When you come to a stop, shift down through the gears as you slow, and keep every change gradual, because an abrupt change of power at the rear wheel can cause a skid (Oregon DMV).

Step 6: Slow riding, a figure of eight and U-turns

Slow control is where clutch skill pays off. The UK syllabus has learners ride slowly, through a figure of eight and through a U-turn under full control while changing gear smoothly (GOV.UK, CBT element C). In slow, tight turns the Oregon manual recommends leaning the bike while keeping your body upright (Oregon DMV). Vietnamese learners face similar tasks: in the format that applies from March 2027, the first A1 riding test under Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA starts with a figure of eight, followed by a straight line, a lane with obstacle lines and a rough surface, and putting a foot down or stalling costs points.

Step 7: Start on a hill

Hill starts matter on northern Vietnam's mountain roads. The Oregon manual's routine (Oregon DMV):

1. Hold the bike with the front brake while you start the engine and select first gear. 2. Switch to the rear brake to hold the bike, so your right hand is free for the throttle. 3. Open the throttle a little and release the clutch gradually. 4. Release the rear brake when the bike starts to pull forward; that is the clutch beginning to transmit power. 5. Keep releasing the clutch gradually. If you let it out too quickly, the front wheel can lift, the engine can stall, or both.

Starting uphill is harder than on flat ground, and there is always a risk of rolling back into the vehicle behind you.

If the engine stalls

Stalls are a normal part of learning. Pull the clutch in, hold the bike with the brakes, select first gear or neutral and restart. On a slope, go back to the hill-start routine instead of trying to roll away. If the bike keeps stalling or will not restart, our guide on what to do if your rental bike breaks down explains the next steps.

A one-week plan before you fly

The plan below is a suggestion; spread it over more days if you need to, ideally with an instructor.

  • Day 1: controls, neutral, starting and stopping the engine, finding the friction zone.
  • Day 2: moving off and stopping in a straight line, using both brakes.
  • Day 3: shifting up to second and third gear and back down.
  • Day 4: slow riding, a figure of eight and U-turns.
  • Day 5: hill starts and stall recovery.
  • Days 6 and 7: short rides on quiet roads with a trainer, practising junctions.

Using these skills in Vietnam

In stop-start city traffic you will spend a lot of time in first gear, using the friction zone to creep forward. Look ahead, keep both hands ready on the levers and leave space in front. Our guide to Hanoi traffic for first-time riders explains how the flow works. On mountain roads, choose your gear before a climb or a bend rather than in the middle of it. Before your first ride, check the clutch, brakes and lights with our rental pickup checklist.

In short

Learn clutch control at home, not on a mountain pass. Start with the controls and the friction zone, then practise starts, stops, upshifts, downshifts, slow riding and hill starts, as official training material from the UK and Oregon describes (GOV.UK, Oregon DMV). Check that your licence covers the engine size, and only rent a manual bike once these steps feel routine.
