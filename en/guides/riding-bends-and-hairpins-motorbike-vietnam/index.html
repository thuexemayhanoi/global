Riding Bends and Hairpins in Vietnam

How to ride bends and hairpins safely on a scooter in northern Vietnam: slowing before the bend, where to look, how to lean, the W.201 and W.202 warning signs and what the law requires in bends.

The mountain roads of northern Vietnam are some of the finest motorbike routes in Asia, and much of their length is bends: long sweepers along valley sides, tight hairpins on the passes, whole staircases of switchbacks. For novices this is the most demanding riding there is, because many falls start with a bend taken too fast or with the rider looking at the wrong spot. This guide explains cornering technique as the Oregon motorcycle manual teaches it, the Vietnamese warning signs for dangerous bends and what the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety requires in them. For an overview of riding safely, see our topic page on safer riding and breakdown support.

Four steps through every bend

The Oregon manual notes that new riders in particular often take bends too fast. It advises reducing speed before the bend and then holding it, and breaks cornering into four steps (Oregon DMV, Section 4):

1. Slow: reduce speed before the bend by closing the throttle and, if needed, using both brakes. 2. Look: look through the bend to where you want to go. Turn your head, not your shoulders, and keep your eyes level with the horizon. 3. Roll: in the bend, roll on a little throttle to keep a steady speed or accelerate gently. That keeps the bike stable. 4. Press: to turn, the bike has to lean, and you make it lean by pressing the handgrip on the side of the turn slightly forward: press left, lean left, go left. The higher the speed or the tighter the bend, the more the bike leans.

For a novice the first step matters most: lose speed before the bend, not halfway round it.

Leaning with the bike

In normal bends, rider and bike lean together at the same angle. In slow, tight turns, the manual advises leaning only the bike and keeping your body upright (Oregon DMV). A passenger should lean with you and not fight it; talk about that before the first mountain stage. Our guide to how many people may ride one motorbike explains the passenger rules.

If you have to brake in a bend

If an obstacle appears mid-bend, apply both brakes gently while gradually bringing the bike upright. The more upright it is, the harder you can brake; too much or too sudden pressure can start a skid (Oregon DMV). On a wet road there is even less grip to spare; our guide to riding in the rainy season has tips for the rain.

The warning signs for dangerous bends

The national road sign standard QCVN 41:2024/BGTVT has its own warning signs for dangerous bends:

  • W.201a and W.201b: dangerous bend to the left and to the right.
  • W.202a and W.202b: a series of dangerous bends.

Under the standard, a bend counts as dangerous on flat terrain when the road turns through 45 degrees or more or has a radius of 100 metres or less, and in the mountains when it turns through 45 degrees or more or has a radius of 40 metres or less (QCVN 41:2024/BGTVT, Annex C.1). When you see one of these signs, the bend is already confirmed: brake now, not as you turn in.

Before blind bends you will often also see the mandatory sign R.309 "Ấn còi", which requires you to sound the horn as you pass it (QCVN 41:2024/BGTVT). Our guide to horn rules explains when else the horn is allowed.

What the law requires in bends

Article 12(3) of Law 36/2024/QH15 requires riders to watch, slow down or stop when turning and where visibility is limited (point c), and at bends, winding roads, passes and slopes (point d). Overtaking on bends with limited visibility is banned by Article 14(6), as is making a U-turn under Article 15(4) and stopping under Article 18(4)(b).

Stay on your own side of the road, too. A rider who cuts a left-hand bend and drifts into the oncoming lane is not on the correct part of the road; under Article 7(3)(d) of Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP that costs 600,000 to 800,000 VND, and above all it is potentially fatal, because oncoming traffic beyond the bend is invisible.

Common mistakes on switchbacks

  • Going in too fast. If you only notice you are too fast once you are in the bend, you end up braking while leaned over. Better to take a bend too slowly than too fast.
  • Staring at the hazard. If you look at the drop or the ditch, that is where you go. Look at the exit of the bend.
  • Expecting others to stay in lane. On switchbacks, lorries and buses often swing well into your half. Keep to the right and expect them.
  • Too fast downhill. On descents the scooter gains speed on its own. Brake in good time before every hairpin.
  • Checking the map. Looking at your phone on a switchback means missing your turn-in point. Stop somewhere safe and visible to check the route.
  • Gravel and wet. Sand and gravel collect in bends, and after rain the surface is slippery. Ride even more slowly then and lean the bike less.

Our guide to narrow mountain roads explains who gives way when the road is too narrow for two.

With a passenger, luggage or after dark

With a passenger or a fully loaded rack, the scooter responds more slowly, needs more room in the bend and a longer stopping distance before it. Approach bends more slowly and avoid sudden steering inputs.

At night, your headlight shines straight ahead into empty space while the road is already turning, so you see the shape of the bend later than in daylight. Plan mountain stages so that you arrive before dark; the law requires your headlight on from 18:00 to 06:00 and in fog anyway (Article 20 of the law). Our guide to riding at night has more.

Practise before the mountains

If you are still unsure, practise tight turns in an empty car park. UK compulsory basic training includes a figure of eight and a U-turn as required exercises (GOV.UK, CBT element C). Ride a large figure of eight slowly, make it smaller step by step and make sure your eyes are always already where you want to go.

In short

Lose speed before the bend, look through it, keep a steady light throttle and press gently on the handlebar to turn in (Oregon DMV). Signs W.201 and W.202 warn of dangerous bends, and at R.309 you sound the horn (QCVN 41:2024/BGTVT). On bends with limited visibility: slow down, do not overtake, do not stop (Articles 12, 14 and 18).
