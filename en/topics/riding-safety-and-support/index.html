Safer Riding and Breakdown Support in Hanoi

Riding a rented motorbike safely in Hanoi: helmet and zero-alcohol rules with current fines, speed limits, how traffic really flows, what to do about breakdowns and theft, and the steps after an accident.

Renting a motorbike is the fastest way to see Hanoi, and also the riskiest thing most visitors do during their trip. The UK Foreign Office says plainly that travelling by motorbike in Vietnam is much riskier than in the UK, that accidents are common and have killed and injured British nationals, and that you should not hire a motorbike if you are not an experienced rider. This guide is for people who have weighed that warning and still want to ride: it explains the rules that are actually enforced in Hanoi, how the traffic really moves, what to do when the bike breaks down or disappears, and the exact steps and numbers to use after an accident. For the mechanical check at pickup, use our Hanoi motorbike rental pickup checklist.

Before you ride: papers, helmet, insurance

Safety starts before the engine does. According to GOV.UK, you need both the 1968 version of the international driving permit and your national driving licence with you while riding a motorbike in Vietnam, it is illegal to ride without third-party insurance, and you should not use your passport as a deposit for hiring vehicles. Riders from countries that only issue 1949-convention permits are in a weaker position: the U.S. Department of State states that U.S. driver's licenses and international driving permits do not work in Vietnam and that riding without a Vietnamese licence can lead to fines or arrest. Whatever your passport, make sure the motorcycle category is actually printed on your permit; a car-only licence gives you no right to a 110 cc scooter.

Wear a helmet with the chin strap fastened on every ride, including as a passenger. GOV.UK notes that it is illegal for drivers and passengers to ride without a helmet, and the thin half-shell helmets handed out by many rental shops offer little protection; a proper open-face helmet with a visor costs little in Hanoi and also keeps dust, rain and insects out of your eyes. Read your travel insurance policy before you rent: many policies exclude motorised two-wheelers or only cover them with a valid licence for the engine size you ride. The FCDO advice is to make sure your travel insurance covers your planned activity, and that includes medical evacuation, because serious injuries outside the big cities are hard to treat locally.

The rules Hanoi police actually enforce

Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, in force since 1 January 2025, raised fines sharply, and the State Department observes that traffic police now have cameras and speed detectors and that enforcement in Hanoi is tightening. Three rules matter most to a rented scooter. First, helmets: according to the legal newspaper Luật sư Việt Nam, riding without a helmet or with the strap unfastened costs 400,000 to 600,000 VND, running a red light costs 4,000,000 to 6,000,000 VND, and riding on the pavement also costs 4,000,000 to 6,000,000 VND. Second, alcohol: Vietnam applies a zero limit, and GOV.UK warns that any alcohol in your system can mean a fine and possible imprisonment. The fines are tiered, as the Cần Thơ Department of Justice sets out: up to 50 mg per 100 ml of blood or 0.25 mg per litre of breath brings 2,000,000 to 3,000,000 VND and four licence points, 50 to 80 mg brings 6,000,000 to 8,000,000 VND and ten points, and above 80 mg brings 8,000,000 to 10,000,000 VND plus a licence suspension of 22 to 24 months. One beer with dinner is enough for the lowest tier.

Third, speed. Under Article 6 of Circular 38/2024/TT-BGTVT, summarised by THƯ VIỆN PHÁP LUẬT, motorbikes in built-up areas may do at most 60 km/h on divided roads or one-way roads with two or more lanes and 50 km/h on two-way roads or single-lane one-way roads; outside built-up areas the limits are 70 and 60 km/h, and mopeds under 50 cc are capped at 40 km/h everywhere. Exceeding the limit by 10 to 20 km/h costs 800,000 to 1,000,000 VND, and by more than 20 km/h 6,000,000 to 8,000,000 VND. Posted signs in Hanoi are often lower than these ceilings, and motorbikes are banned from the elevated expressway sections and several bridges, so follow the signs rather than the general rule. Keep your phone in a holder or a pocket; holding it while riding is also fined.

How Hanoi traffic really works

Hanoi traffic looks chaotic but follows its own logic, which the State Department describes as organised chaos in which drivers rely on watching each other rather than strictly following rules. Traffic drives on the right; motorbikes stay to the right, cars and buses take the left lanes. Ride smoothly and predictably: sudden lane changes and hard braking cause more trouble than slow, steady progress. At junctions, nobody expects you to stop dead and wait for a gap; slow down, make eye contact, and roll through with the flow of other bikes. Short horn taps are information, not aggression, and simply mean someone is beside or behind you. Expect vehicles to pull out of side streets without looking and scooters to ride against the flow along the kerb.

The real dangers are city buses swinging left as they pull away from stops, trucks with large blind spots, slick manhole covers and painted lines in the rain, and the on-ramps of elevated roads where motorbikes are prohibited. Night riding multiplies the risk; avoid unlit outer districts after dinner and wear light clothing. In the rainy season, roughly May to September, streets can flood knee-deep within half an hour. Do not ride through underpasses or flooded roads, because an engine that swallows water is a repair the renter usually pays for. Keep a rain poncho with a clear panel for the headlight under the seat, along with water for the heat.

Breakdowns, flat tyres and theft

The most common failures are a flat tyre, a dead battery on older scooters and a lost key. Almost every main street in Hanoi has a tiny repair stall marked "sửa xe" or "vá xe", where a puncture is patched in minutes; ask the price before the work starts and pay in cash. For anything beyond a tube or a bulb, call the rental shop first: most contracts specify who pays for repairs and whether a replacement bike will be delivered, and unauthorised repairs are a classic source of disputes at return time. Save the shop's phone number, the plate number and a photo of the contract on your phone before you leave the shop.

If the bike dies, push it to the kerb, never leave it on a bridge approach or in a bend, and never leave it unattended. Two-wheeler theft is common in Hanoi, so use guarded parking during the day, recognisable by "gửi xe" signs, always take the key, engage the steering lock and do not leave a helmet or bag on the bike. If the bike is stolen, report it to the police immediately and inform the rental shop the same hour, because most contracts make the renter liable for the valued price of a lost vehicle.

After an accident: step by step

If you are involved in a crash, the State Department lists the legal obligations: stop immediately, help injured people, stay at the scene, keep everything as it is until police arrive, and report the accident to police. Dial 113 for the police and 115 for an ambulance. Photograph the vehicles, plates, damage and surroundings, note the names and numbers of witnesses, and call the rental shop at once, because its contract and insurance decide how the vehicle damage is handled. Do not sign anything you cannot read and do not pay anyone under pressure at the roadside. Minor collisions are often settled by agreement between the parties in Vietnam, but an agreement without a police report can be used against you later.

Take the legal exposure seriously. GOV.UK warns that if you are involved in an accident you could face criminal charges and may need to pay compensation to injured people even if the injuries are minor, and that a travel ban is possible while the investigation is carried out; it advises offering the police full cooperation and informing the British Embassy in Hanoi. Riders of any nationality should contact their own embassy, notify their insurer within the policy deadline and keep every receipt and document.

Heat, health and medical care

Riding in Hanoi is more tiring than it looks: heat, dust, noise and constant concentration wear you down. Ride shorter stretches in the midday heat, drink plenty of water, cover your arms and use sunscreen, and take breaks. A dust mask is normal and sensible in Hanoi. The World Health Organization's road traffic injuries fact sheet identifies riders of motorised two-wheelers as among the most vulnerable road users and names speed, alcohol and the absence of helmets as the leading risk factors; those three are entirely within your control as a renter.

Hanoi has hospitals and international clinics with English-speaking staff, but care thins out quickly outside the city. Before a day trip into the countryside, check where the nearest hospital is, carry your insurance details and tell someone your route. Ride with a fastened helmet, sober, within the limits and with valid papers, and the motorbike becomes what it should be in Hanoi: the best seat in the city rather than its biggest risk.
