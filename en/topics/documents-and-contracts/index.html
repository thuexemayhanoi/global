Rental documents and contracts when hiring a motorbike in Hanoi

Documents and contracts for renting a motorbike in Hanoi: ID and passport practice, IDP and licence rules, what a written rental contract must contain, and the bike papers you must carry.

Renting a motorbike in Hanoi is usually a fast, informal-looking transaction, but the paperwork around it decides what happens when something goes wrong. Which identity documents you hand over, what the rental contract says, and which vehicle papers ride with you determine your legal position after a breakdown, a fine, a theft or a crash. This guide explains what to prepare before you reach the counter, what a written rental agreement should contain, which vehicle documents must travel with the bike, and how the compulsory insurance system in Vietnam treats renters. For the physical condition checks that pair with the paperwork, work through the Hanoi rental handover checklist before you sign anything.

Identity documents the rental shop will ask for

Vietnamese renters are normally asked to leave one personal identity document, usually the national identity card (CCCD), plus a cash deposit whose amount depends on the vehicle class. Foreign visitors are asked for a passport, and some shops additionally record a return flight or hotel booking as an informal guarantee. Practices differ between shops: one Hanoi rental service publishes a policy of holding an ID card or passport with a cash deposit of about VND 3,000,000 for underbone bikes and VND 5,000,000 for scooters and manual motorbikes, while also offering customers who prefer not to leave an identity document a higher cash deposit instead, as described on its published rental policy page.

Two cautions apply. First, handing over your original passport for the whole rental period is legally sensitive: many embassies advise carrying your passport at all times in Vietnam, and you may need it for hotel check-in, money exchange or SIM registration. Ask whether a certified copy plus a higher deposit is acceptable, and get whatever is agreed written into the contract. Second, an identity document held by the shop is not a driving licence: being able to rent a bike does not mean you are legally entitled to ride it.

Driving licence rules that shape the contract

Since 1 January 2025, Vietnam's licence framework draws a hard line at engine capacity. Category A1 covers two-wheeled motorcycles up to 125 cc, or electric motorcycles up to 11 kW; anything larger requires category A. Foreign riders must carry a 1968 Vienna Convention International Driving Permit together with their original national licence, and the IDP only extends the entitlements the national licence already contains, so a car-only licence does not legitimise riding a motorbike. Legal guides for foreign riders, such as the Rentabike Vietnam licence guide, stress checking the engine size of the rental bike against your licence category before paying a deposit.

For the rental contract this has a practical consequence: a serious shop will refuse to hand over a bike to a rider without a valid licence, because under Decree 168/2024 a shop that knowingly hands a bike to an unlicensed rider exposes itself to substantial administrative fines. If a shop shows no interest whatsoever in your licence, treat that as a warning sign about how its contract will behave after an accident. Riders who cannot meet the licence requirements should use licensed driver services or other transport instead of self-riding.

What a written rental contract should contain

Insist on a written contract, even a simple one, and read it before signing. A usable Hanoi motorbike rental agreement should state: the renter's and owner's identification details; the exact bike with its registration plate and frame number; the rental period with start and end date and time; the rental fee and what it includes; the deposit amount, the form it is held in, and the deadline for its return; the fuel level at handover; the condition of the bike including existing scratches, ideally documented with photos attached or referenced; liability rules for breakdowns, accidents, theft and traffic fines; and a contact number for roadside problems. Vietnamese rental shops commonly use a civil-contract template, and reputable ones hand you a copy to keep until you return the bike.

Pay particular attention to three clauses. The deposit clause should say when and how the deposit comes back, not only that it exists. The damage clause should distinguish normal wear from payable damage; if it is silent, ask for the distinction in writing. The breakdown clause should say who pays when the bike fails mechanically, and whether support is available outside the city. A shop that refuses any written record is asking you to carry all the risk of the transaction, which is reason enough to walk to the next shop.

Vehicle papers that must ride with the bike

When you receive the bike, ask for the registration certificate (giấy đăng ký xe) or a legible copy, and for the compulsory civil liability insurance certificate (bảo hiểm trách nhiệm dân sự bắt buộc). In Vietnam, compulsory third-party liability insurance is a legal requirement for every motorbike circulating in traffic, and being unable to present a valid certificate at a police check is itself an administrative violation. Under Decree 67/2023, which sets the current insurance framework, annual premiums for motorbikes are modest, on the order of VND 55,000 to 60,000 plus VAT for common classes, and the liability coverage limit for damage caused by a two-wheeled motorbike is VND 50,000,000 per accident, as summarised in Vietnamese insurance reporting on the 2026 rates.

Understand what this insurance is and is not: it covers the owner's liability towards third parties, not damage to the rental bike itself and not your medical costs. Some rental shops include voluntary physical-damage cover for their fleet; most do not, and the contract will make you liable for the bike. Assume the full value of the bike is on your shoulders unless the contract says otherwise, and ride accordingly.

Documents at handover, documents on the road

Photograph every page of the contract, the bike's papers, the bike from four sides, the odometer and the fuel gauge, and keep the images on your phone with a cloud backup. If you are stopped by traffic police, the documents that matter are your driving licence and IDP if you are foreign, your passport or ID, and the bike's registration and insurance certificate; hand copies rather than originals where possible and stay calm and cooperative. If the bike is impounded for a paperwork violation that existed before your rental, that is exactly the scenario the liability clause in your contract should have addressed, which is why you read it at the counter and not at the impound lot.

When returning the bike, repeat the handover ritual in reverse: inspect together, photograph the same four sides, note the fuel level, obtain the shop's confirmation of the bike's condition in writing or in a message thread, and only then collect your deposit and identity document. Count the deposit before leaving the counter. If the shop deducts anything, the deduction should map to a specific clause in the contract you both signed.

Checklist before you sign

Bring it together in one pass: your identity document and licence or IDP checked against the bike's engine size; a written contract with fee, deposit, damage, breakdown and contact clauses; bike papers photographed; bike condition documented; helmets checked; and the handover checklist completed on the spot. If any item cannot be satisfied, you have learned something cheap at the counter instead of expensive on the road. Once the paperwork is clean, the rest of riding in Hanoi is a traffic problem, and you can prepare for that part through the English topic hub on this site.
